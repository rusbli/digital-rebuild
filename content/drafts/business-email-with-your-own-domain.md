"Please send the quote to dave.plumbing1987@..."

There's nothing wrong with a free email address. Lots of good businesses use one. But when a customer is choosing between two quotes, the one from hello@davesplumbing.co.uk quietly looks more established.

It's a small thing that costs little and makes you look like a proper business. Here's what it involves.

> **Quick answer:** Business email with your own domain (like hello@yourbusiness.co.uk) needs two things: a domain name you own, and an email service connected to it. Common options are a business plan from a big provider such as Google or Microsoft, or email included with your web hosting. Choose based on how you work, make sure the domain and email account are in your name, and set up the records that help your emails avoid spam folders.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Is it worth paying for when free email works fine?
- Which kind of email service should I choose?
- What's the step most people miss that sends their emails to spam?

## Why it's worth it

Here's the first question. A business email address helps in a few quiet ways:

- **Trust.** It shows you've invested in your business. On a quote or invoice, it looks more established.
- **Consistency.** Your email, website and business cards all carry the same name.
- **Memorability.** hello@yourbusiness.co.uk is easier to remember than a free address with numbers in it.
- **Control.** If someone joins or leaves your business, you can create or close their address without anyone losing access to the business's emails.

It also stops a common problem: when the business email is someone's personal account, and that person leaves.

## What you need

1. **A domain name you own.** If you don't have one yet, my guide to [choosing a domain name](/blog/choosing-a-domain-name-uk/) covers it.
2. **An email service** connected to that domain.

You don't need a website. Plenty of businesses set up email on their domain first and add a website later.

## Your options

Here's the second question. There are three common routes:

| Option | Good for | Things to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A business plan from a big provider (such as Google Workspace or Microsoft 365) | People who already like Gmail or Outlook, want shared calendars, or want reliable apps on every device | Monthly cost per user; check current prices on the provider's site |
| Email included with your web hosting | Very small businesses wanting the simplest, cheapest option | Storage limits, reliability and how easy it is to use on your phone |
| A specialist email provider | People who want something in between, or privacy-focused options | Features, support and how it connects to your phone |

There's no single right answer. If you already live in Gmail or Outlook, the big providers' business plans feel familiar. If you only need one simple inbox, email with your hosting might be enough.

Prices change, so check the current figures on each provider's own page rather than relying on an old comparison.

## Before you choose, check these

- **How many addresses do you need?** Some services charge per person; others allow several addresses or aliases on one inbox.
- **Do you need shared calendars or file storage?**
- **Will it work in the app you already use on your phone?**
- **How much storage do you get?** Trades who email lots of photos fill inboxes quickly.
- **What happens to your old emails?** Most services can import them.

## The step most people miss

Here's the third question. Once your email is set up, there are a few settings on your domain that tell other email systems your messages are genuine. They're usually called **SPF**, **DKIM** and **DMARC**.

You don't need to understand how they work. You do need them set up. Without them, your emails are more likely to land in customers' spam folders, which is a disaster when you're sending quotes.

Your email provider will give you the exact records to add. If someone else manages your domain, send them the instructions. It's a short job, and worth checking even if your email "works", because sending and arriving in the inbox aren't the same thing.

A simple test: send an email to a friend who uses a different email service and ask whether it landed in their inbox or their spam.

## Ownership: set it up in your name

As with your domain, the email account should be in your name, or your business's name, with you holding the main login.

If a web designer sets it up for you, ask to be the administrator. Otherwise, if you part ways, you could lose access to years of customer emails. My guide on [who owns your website](/blog/who-owns-my-website/) covers what to check.

## Switching from a free address

You don't have to switch overnight:

1. Set up the new address.
2. Forward your old address to the new one, or check both for a while.
3. Update your website, Google Business Profile, invoices, van and social pages.
4. Add a line to your old address's signature for a few months: "We've moved to hello@yourbusiness.co.uk."

Most people switch over a few weeks without missing a message.

## Make the most of it

A few small touches:

- **Use a sensible main address.** hello@, info@ or your first name all work.
- **Add a simple signature** with your name, business, phone and website. Skip the giant logo images.
- **Use the same address everywhere.** Your website, profile, invoices and quotes should all match.
