Ask around and someone will tell you WordPress is the only sensible choice for a business website. Someone else will tell you it's a nightmare of updates and hacks.

Both have a point. WordPress is hugely popular, flexible and well supported. It's also a system with lots of moving parts that need looking after.

Whether it's right for you depends on what you need your website to do and who's going to look after it.

> **Quick answer:** WordPress is a flexible, widely used system that's good if you want to edit lots of content yourself, run a blog, or need features that plugins provide. The software is free, but you pay for hosting, often for themes and plugins, and for ongoing updates and security. For a small brochure site that rarely changes, a simpler, leaner build may be faster, cheaper to look after and less to go wrong.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What does WordPress actually cost to run?
- What's the maintenance burden?
- When is something simpler a better fit?

## What WordPress is

WordPress is a content management system: software that runs your website and gives you a dashboard to edit pages, posts and images.

There are two versions that people often confuse:

- **WordPress software** (from WordPress.org), which you install on your own hosting. This is what most people mean.
- **WordPress.com**, a hosted service that runs WordPress for you, with its own plans.

This guide is mainly about the first.

## What it's good at

- **Editing content yourself.** Pages and posts can be changed through a familiar editor.
- **Blogging.** It started as a blogging platform and it's still excellent at it.
- **Flexibility.** Thousands of themes and plugins add features, from booking forms to online shops.
- **Moving.** Your site isn't locked into one company; you can move hosts.
- **Lots of help available.** Many designers and developers work with it.

## What it costs to run

Here's the first question. The software is free, but a WordPress site usually involves:

| Cost | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Domain name | Yearly |
| Hosting | Monthly or yearly; quality varies widely |
| Premium theme | Optional, often a one-off or yearly licence |
| Premium plugins | Some charge yearly licences |
| Maintenance | Updates, backups, security; your time or a care plan |
| Design and build | If someone builds it for you |

The cost that catches people out is maintenance. My guide to [what a small business website costs](/blog/small-business-website-cost-uk/) explains how to add up yearly costs over three years.

## The maintenance burden

Here's the second question. A WordPress site has three layers that all get updates:

1. WordPress itself.
2. Your theme.
3. Every plugin.

Updates fix bugs and security holes, so skipping them is risky. But updates can also clash with each other and break things, so someone needs to apply them carefully, with backups in place.

The more plugins a site has, the more there is to update, and the more there is to go wrong. Heavy themes and lots of plugins can also slow the site down.

That's why many WordPress sites come with a monthly care plan. My guide to [website care plans](/blog/website-care-plan-cost/) covers what a good one should include.

## When something simpler fits better

Here's the third question. WordPress can be more than a small business needs. A simpler option may suit you better if:

- **Your site is a few pages** that rarely change.
- **You don't plan to blog** or edit content often.
- **You don't want ongoing updates** to worry about.
- **Speed matters a lot** to you.

In that situation, the options are:

- **A website builder** like Wix or Squarespace, where the platform handles updates. My guide to [Wix, Squarespace or a custom website](/blog/wix-squarespace-or-custom-website/) compares them.
- **A lean, hand-coded site**, which is what I build. There's no database or plugins to update, so it's fast and needs little maintenance. The trade-off is that you'd usually ask your designer to make changes, rather than editing it yourself.

## The aha: choose based on who will update it

The most useful question isn't "which platform is best?" It's "who is going to keep this website up to date, and how often?"

- If **you** want to edit things weekly, a system with a friendly editor (WordPress or a builder) makes sense.
- If **someone else** will make occasional changes, a leaner site may be faster and cheaper to look after.
- If **nobody** will touch it for a year, choose something that won't need constant updates to stay safe.

Many neglected WordPress sites are neglected because nobody was ever really responsible for updating them.

## If you already have WordPress

You don't need to switch. Instead:

1. Make sure WordPress, your theme and plugins are updated.
2. Remove plugins you don't use.
3. Make sure backups run and can be restored.
4. Check speed on mobile.
5. Decide who's responsible for updates.

## A quick decision guide

- **Want a blog and lots of self-editing?** WordPress is a strong choice.
- **Want to do it all yourself, simply?** A builder may be easier.
- **Want a few fast pages that just work?** A lean custom site may suit you best.

And whichever you choose, make sure the domain and accounts are in your name.
