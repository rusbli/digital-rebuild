You've seen them on almost every website: the box that pops up asking you to accept cookies.

So you assume your small business website needs one too. Maybe your web designer added one. Maybe you've been told you'll be fined without it.

The real answer depends on what your website actually does. Some small business sites need a proper consent banner. Some don't. And the rules changed in 2026, which has confused things further.

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. But here's how to work out where you stand, using the regulator's own guidance.

> **Quick answer:** UK websites need consent before setting cookies or similar technologies, unless an exception applies. The ICO's current guidance includes exceptions for things that are strictly necessary and, since 2026, for some statistical analytics used only to improve your own site, provided you give clear information and an easy way to object. If your site uses advertising, social media or tracking cookies, you'll need a consent banner. Start by finding out which cookies your site actually sets.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Which cookies need consent and which don't?
- What changed in 2026?
- How do I find out what my site is doing?

## What the law covers

The rules come from the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations, known as PECR, and they're enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office.

They don't only cover "cookies" in the narrow sense. They cover any technology that stores information on, or reads information from, someone's device. The ICO now calls these **storage and access technologies**. That includes cookies, tracking pixels and similar tools.

The basic rule: you need the person's consent, unless an exception applies.

## The exceptions, in plain English

Here's the first question. The ICO's guidance on [what the exceptions are](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/what-are-the-exceptions/) sets out when you don't need consent.

The ones most relevant to small business websites are:

- **Strictly necessary.** Things the website genuinely can't work without for something the visitor asked for, such as keeping items in a shopping basket.
- **Statistical purposes.** Collecting statistics about visitors, solely to improve your site or service, with clear information and an easy way to object.
- **Others** in the ICO's list, such as security and appearance preferences, each with their own conditions.

The important word across all of them is **solely**. The ICO says an exception only applies if the storage or access is for that purpose alone. If the same cookie is also used for something else, like advertising, you need consent.

## What changed in 2026

Here's the second question. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 amended PECR, adding new exceptions, and the ICO updated its guidance to match.

The headline change for small businesses is the **statistical purposes** exception. In short, some analytics can now be used without asking first, as long as:

- The data is used **only** to understand and improve your own website.
- You explain clearly what you use.
- People have an easy way to object.

Whether your particular analytics tool qualifies depends on how it's set up and what the provider does with the data. If your analytics shares data for other purposes, such as advertising, the exception won't cover it. Check your tool's settings and terms against the ICO's guidance rather than assuming.

## Find out what your website actually does

Here's the third question, and it's the step most people skip. Before deciding anything, find out which cookies and trackers your site sets.

A simple way to look:

1. Open your website in Chrome on a computer.
2. Right-click, choose **Inspect**, then open the **Application** tab.
3. Look under **Cookies** for your site.

Then think about what's on your site that might set its own:

- **Analytics** (such as Google Analytics).
- **Embedded videos** from YouTube or similar.
- **Social media buttons or feeds.**
- **Advertising or remarketing pixels** (such as Facebook or Google Ads tags).
- **Chat widgets and booking tools.**
- **Embedded maps.**

If you're not sure, ask your web designer for a list of every third-party tool on the site and what each one does.

## Three common situations

### A simple brochure site with no tracking

Just pages, a contact form, maybe a map. If nothing sets non-essential cookies, you may not need a consent banner at all. You still need to tell people what you do with their information, usually in a privacy notice.

### A site with analytics only

You may fall within the statistical purposes exception, if your set-up meets its conditions. If not, you need consent before the analytics runs. Either way, you must explain what you use and how to object.

### A site with advertising, social or remarketing tags

These generally need consent. That means a proper banner that:

- Doesn't set those cookies until the person agrees.
- Makes refusing as easy as accepting.
- Doesn't pre-tick boxes.

The ICO is clear that you must not switch on non-exempt technologies before consent.

## The aha: the banner isn't the goal

Many sites have a banner that pops up, but the tracking runs anyway, whatever the visitor clicks. That's the worst of both worlds: an annoying box and no actual compliance.

The real question isn't "do I have a banner?" It's "does my site only set non-exempt cookies after someone agrees?"

Sometimes the simplest answer is to remove tools you don't need. Fewer third-party scripts often means a faster site, a simpler privacy notice and no banner.

## What you still need either way

Even with no banner, you need to be open about what you collect. If you have a contact form, you're collecting personal information, and people should be told what you do with it. That's usually covered by a privacy notice linked from your footer and your form.

## A practical checklist

1. List every tool on your site that could set cookies or trackers.
2. Decide which ones you really need. Remove the rest.
3. Check each remaining one against the ICO's exceptions.
4. If anything needs consent, use a banner that blocks it until people agree and makes refusing easy.
5. Explain what you use in a cookie or privacy notice.
6. Recheck whenever you add a new tool.
