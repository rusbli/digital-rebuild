Picture the person on the other end of a plumbing search.

Water is coming through the kitchen ceiling. Or the boiler's dead and it's December. Or they've been putting off a new bathroom for three years and have finally decided to get quotes.

Those are three very different people, and a good plumber's website speaks to all of them without making any of them work for it.

This guide goes through what to put on each page, in the order customers look for it. It's the same thinking I use when I build sites for trades, and it works for heating engineers and bathroom fitters too.

> **Quick answer:** A plumber's website needs to tell a stressed visitor, within seconds, what you do, where you cover, and how to reach you now. Then it needs to prove you're safe to let into their home: real photos, genuine reviews, your Gas Safe number if you do gas work, and an honest idea of price. Give each main service its own page so you can be found for it.

Three questions to settle along the way:

- Should emergency and planned work sit on the same page?
- Do you really need to show prices?
- What can you safely leave off?

## Start with the emergency visitor

The person with water coming through the ceiling is the most likely to call, and the least patient.

On a phone, before they scroll, they need:

- **What you do.** "Plumber and heating engineer", not a slogan.
- **Where.** "Barry, Penarth and Cardiff", or whatever you genuinely cover.
- **Whether you do emergencies.** And if so, when. "Same-day callouts, Monday to Saturday" is honest and useful. "24/7" is only worth saying if you really answer at 3am.
- **A phone number they can tap.** Big, at the top, on every page.

If the site makes them hunt for any of these, they'll be on the next website in seconds. That's the most common leak I see, and my guide on [websites that get visitors but no enquiries](/blog/website-not-getting-enquiries/) goes into how to fix it.

## Give each main service its own page

Here's the answer to the first question. Emergency work and planned work deserve separate pages.

The burst-pipe visitor and the new-bathroom visitor want different things:

| Visitor | What they want to know | What makes them call |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency repair | Can you come today? How much is a callout? | A phone number and a clear yes |
| Boiler service or repair | Are you Gas Safe? Which boilers do you work on? | Registration number, makes covered, price guide |
| Boiler installation | What will it cost? How long? What warranty? | A clear process and examples of past installs |
| Bathroom fitting | Can I see your work? Do you handle tiling and electrics? | A gallery and a step-by-step of how a job runs |

Separate pages help Google too. A page about boiler installations in the Vale of Glamorgan has a much better chance of appearing for that search than a single line on a general services page.

Most plumbing businesses only need a handful of these pages. Write one for each service that brings in real money, and skip the ones you rarely do.

## The trust section: why should I let you into my home?

For a lot of customers, this is the real decision. They're letting a stranger into their house, often while they're at work.

Show them what they need to feel safe:

- **Gas Safe registration.** If you do gas work, show your registration number so customers can check it on the Gas Safe Register. It's a legal requirement for gas work, and customers who know that will look for it.
- **Real photos.** You, your van, your work. Stock photos of a smiling man with a spanner fool nobody.
- **Genuine reviews.** Link to your Google reviews, or show a few recent ones with the reviewer's first name and area. Never invent them, and never edit them to sound better.
- **Insurance and guarantees.** If you carry public liability insurance or guarantee your work, say so plainly.
- **Who you are.** A short paragraph about you, how long you've been working, and the area you're from.

None of this needs fancy design. It needs to be true and easy to find.

## Prices: the question everyone's asking

Here's the second question. Should you show prices?

I'd say yes, wherever you can. Customers want a rough idea before they ring, and if they can't find one, they often ring someone who gives them one.

You don't need a full price list. Options that work:

- **A call-out fee.** "£X call-out, including the first half hour."
- **Starting prices.** "Boiler service from £X."
- **Typical ranges.** "Most bathroom installations we do cost between £X and £Y, depending on size and tiling."
- **How you price.** If every job is different, explain that you quote after a visit, whether the visit is free, and how quickly you send the quote.

Use your own real numbers. And date them, or review them every few months, so you're not quoting last year's prices.

## Show your work like a portfolio

Bathroom and kitchen customers buy with their eyes. A gallery of your finished jobs is one of the most persuasive things a plumbing site can have.

A few tips:

- Take photos in good light, after you've cleaned up.
- Before-and-after pairs are especially powerful.
- Add one line of context: "Full bathroom refit in Penarth, walk-in shower and underfloor heating."
- Ask customers' permission before posting photos of their home.

When I put together sample concepts for plumbing sites, such as the [example layouts on the Digital Rebuild homepage](/#why), the work photos always sit near the top. They do more persuading than any amount of text.

## The contact page

Keep it simple:

- Phone number (tappable).
- Email address.
- A short form: name, phone, postcode, and "what's the problem?"
- Your service area, in words.
- Your hours, and what happens out of hours.

A postcode field is worth including. It tells you straight away whether the job's in your area, and it saves a call to find out.

## What you can leave off

Here's the third question. Plenty of plumbing websites carry things that don't help:

- **Long company histories** on the home page. A sentence is plenty; put the rest on the about page.
- **Sliders and carousels.** The important message is often on the slide nobody waits for.
- **Every brand logo you've ever fitted.** List the boiler makes you work on, by all means, but a wall of logos isn't persuasive.
- **Services you don't want to do.** If you hate small dripping-tap jobs, don't advertise them.
- **A blog you'll never update.** An empty news page with one post from four years ago looks worse than no news page at all.

## Connect it to your Google Business Profile

For plumbers, the map results are often where the calls come from. Your website and your Google Business Profile should work as a pair: same name, same phone number, same service area, and a link from the profile to your site.

If you haven't set up your profile properly yet, my [Google Business Profile guide for tradespeople](/blog/google-business-profile-for-trades/) covers it, including how to keep your home address private.

## A simple checklist

Before you call your website finished, check it on your phone:

1. Can I see what you do, where and how to call within five seconds?
2. Does every main service have its own page?
3. Is your Gas Safe number visible, if you do gas work?
4. Are there real photos and genuine reviews?
5. Is there at least a rough idea of price?
6. Does the contact form ask for a postcode, and does it actually send?

Six yeses and you're ahead of a lot of the plumbing websites out there.
