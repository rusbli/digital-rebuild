Someone has read your website. They like what they see. They decide to get in touch.

Then they meet your contact form.

Twelve fields. Half of them required. A dropdown for "How did you hear about us?". A box asking for their full address. A puzzle to prove they're not a robot. They tap send, and nothing obvious happens.

Some of those people try again. Many don't. And you never know they were there.

The contact form is the last step between a visitor and an enquiry, so small improvements here can make a real difference.

> **Quick answer:** Keep the form short: name, phone or email, and a message, plus one or two fields that genuinely help you reply, like a postcode. Make only essential fields required. Show a clear confirmation after sending, and tell people when you'll reply. Test the form monthly from your phone, check your spam folder, and offer your phone number and email alongside it.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Which fields should I keep?
- Why do some enquiries never arrive?
- What should happen after someone presses send?

## Every field is a small hurdle

Each field you add asks the visitor to do a bit more work. On a phone, with a thumb, that adds up quickly.

Some fields are worth it because they help you reply properly. Many aren't.

## Which fields to keep

Here's the first question.

| Field | Keep? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Yes | So you know who you're talking to |
| Phone or email | Yes, at least one | So you can reply. Let people choose which. |
| Message | Yes | So they can tell you what they need |
| Postcode | Often | Tells local businesses instantly if the job's in their area |
| Service needed | Sometimes | A short dropdown can help you prioritise |
| Full address | Rarely | You can ask later if needed |
| Company name | Only for business customers | Pointless and off-putting for homeowners |
| "How did you hear about us?" | Optional | Useful to you, but never make it required |
| Budget | Depends | Useful for bigger projects, but can feel intrusive |

The rule: if you don't need it to send a useful first reply, either remove it or make it optional.

## Make it work well on a phone

Most enquiries start on a phone, so check:

- **Fields are big enough** to tap easily.
- **The right keyboard appears**: number pad for phone numbers, email keyboard for email addresses.
- **Labels stay visible.** Placeholder text that disappears as you type makes people forget what the field was.
- **Error messages are clear** and appear next to the field with the problem.
- **The send button is obvious.**

Fill your own form in on your phone. If anything annoys you, it's annoying your customers too.

## Why some enquiries never arrive

Here's the second question. This is the most expensive problem, because you never see what you've lost.

Common causes:

- **Form emails land in spam.** Your own email system may treat them as suspicious.
- **The form sends to an old address**, like a former employee's or an inbox nobody checks.
- **A website update breaks the form**, and nobody notices.
- **Spam protection is too aggressive** and blocks real people.

The fix is simple and boring: **test your form every month.** Use your phone, use a different email address, and send a real-looking message. If it doesn't arrive within a few minutes, investigate.

It's also worth checking your spam folder regularly, and making sure your email is set up properly. My guide to [business email on your own domain](/blog/business-email-with-your-own-domain/) explains the settings that help emails arrive.

## What should happen after someone presses send?

Here's the third question. The moment after sending matters more than people think.

A good form:

- **Shows a clear confirmation message**: "Thanks, Sarah. Your message has arrived."
- **Says when you'll reply**: "I'll get back to you within one working day."
- **Offers an alternative** for urgent enquiries: "If it's urgent, call 07..."

If possible, also send the person an automatic email confirming you've received their message. It reassures them it worked, and it's one less "did you get my message?" call.

Then reply quickly. A fast reply is often what wins the job.

## The aha: give people a choice

Some people love forms. Some would rather call. Some would rather email from their own inbox so they have a record.

A contact page that offers all three (form, phone number, email address) catches more of them. Hiding your phone number behind a form loses everyone who just wanted to ring.

## Spam without the frustration

Spam is annoying, but protecting against it shouldn't punish real customers. Options that are kinder to humans include:

- A hidden field that bots fill in but people don't see (often called a "honeypot").
- Spam protection that works in the background, without puzzles.

Use puzzles only as a last resort.

## Tell people what you'll do with their details

A contact form collects personal information, so explain what you'll use it for, briefly, next to the form. A line like "I'll only use your details to reply about your enquiry" plus a link to your privacy notice does the job. My guide to [privacy policies for small business websites](/blog/privacy-policy-small-business-website/) covers what the notice should include.

## A quick checklist

1. Only the fields you need, with only essentials required?
2. Easy to fill in on a phone?
3. Clear confirmation message and reply time after sending?
4. Phone number and email shown alongside the form?
5. Tested this month, from a phone, with the message arriving?
6. A short note about what you'll do with their details?
