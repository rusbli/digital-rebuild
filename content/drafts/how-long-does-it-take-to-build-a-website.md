You've decided to get a new website. The obvious next question: how long will it take?

Ask five designers and you'll get answers from "a few days" to "three months". Then, often, it takes longer than whatever you were told.

The build itself is rarely what takes the time. Here's where the time really goes, and how to keep your project moving.

> **Quick answer:** A small brochure website can often be designed and built in a few weeks, if the content is ready and decisions are made quickly. I aim for about three weeks from first call to launch for my standard packages. Online shops, booking systems and larger sites take longer. The most common cause of delay is waiting for content: text, photos, prices and logins. Prepare those early and the timeline usually holds.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What are the stages, and how long does each take?
- What actually causes delays?
- How can I speed things up?

## The stages of a typical small website

Here's the first question. Most small business websites go through roughly these stages:

| Stage | What happens | Who it depends on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery | A call to understand your business and goals | Both of you |
| Content | Gathering text, photos, prices and details | Mostly you |
| Design | A mock-up of how the site will look | Designer, then your feedback |
| Build | Turning the design into a working site | Designer |
| Review | You test it and request changes | You, then designer |
| Launch | Going live, setting up analytics and Search Console | Designer, plus domain access |

For my own [Basic and Starter packages](/#pricing), I aim for about three weeks from first call to launch: a design mock-up within a week, two rounds of changes, a build you review on a test link, then launch.

## What actually causes delays

Here's the second question. Delays rarely come from the design or build itself. They usually come from the stages that depend on the business owner, simply because running a business leaves little spare time.

### Waiting for content

Text, photos, prices, service lists, team bios. It's surprisingly hard to find time to write them when you're busy running a business.

### Slow feedback

A design mock-up sits unread for two weeks because life got busy.

### Changing the brief

"Actually, could we add an online shop?" halfway through can add weeks.

### Access problems

Nobody knows the login to the domain, or the old designer won't respond. My guide on [what to do if your web designer has disappeared](/blog/web-designer-disappeared/) covers this.

### Too many decision-makers

When three people need to approve every change, progress slows.

## How to speed things up

Here's the third question. You can make a big difference before the project even starts.

### 1. Gather content early

Before your first call, start collecting:

- A list of your services, with a sentence or two on each.
- Prices or price ranges.
- Areas you cover.
- Photos of your work, your team and your van or premises.
- Reviews you can use.
- Any logos and brand colours.

### 2. Write in plain words

You don't need polished copy. Plain notes in your own words are perfect, and a good designer can tidy them up. My guide to [describing your services in plain English](/blog/describing-your-services-in-plain-english/) helps.

### 3. Find your logins

Make sure you can access your domain, and know who hosts any existing site.

### 4. Agree a feedback rhythm

Block out time in your diary to review designs within a couple of days.

### 5. Keep the first version focused

Launch with the pages you need now. Add extras later. A good site live this month beats a perfect site live next year.

## The aha: content is the real project

Many people think of a website project as a design job. It's really a content job, with design wrapped around it.

When the content is ready, the design and build usually move quickly. When it isn't, everything waits.

So if you want your website sooner, the most useful thing you can do is start writing today.

## After launch: how long until Google finds it?

Launching isn't the end. Google needs to find and index your pages. Google's [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) says some changes take effect within hours and others can take months.

Setting up Google Search Console and submitting your sitemap helps Google find everything. My [Search Console guide](/blog/google-search-console-for-small-business/) explains how.

## Questions to ask any designer about timing

- What's a realistic timeline from first call to launch?
- What do you need from me, and by when?
- How many rounds of changes are included?
- What happens if I'm late with content?
- What happens after launch?
