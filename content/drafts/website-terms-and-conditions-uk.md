Most small business owners worry about the wrong legal bits of their website.

They spend time copying a long terms and conditions page from somewhere, and forget the simple things that genuinely apply to them, like their company number, or a proper explanation of what happens to the details people send through the contact form.

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. But here's a plain overview of the information UK small business websites commonly need, with links to the official guidance so you can check what applies to you.

> **Quick answer:** Most UK small business websites should clearly show who you are and how to contact you, including an email address and geographic address. Limited companies must show their registered name, number, registered office and place of registration. If you collect personal data, you need a privacy notice, and cookies have their own rules. If you sell online, you need clear terms and must respect consumer rights. Check the GOV.UK and ICO guidance for your situation.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What does every business website commonly need?
- What extra applies to limited companies?
- What changes if I sell online?

## What most business websites need

Here's the first question. The [Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/6) require businesses providing services online to make certain information easily available. For a typical business website, that means making it easy to find:

- **Your business name.**
- **A geographic address** (not just a PO box).
- **Contact details**, including an email address.
- **Your VAT number**, if you're VAT registered.
- **Membership of a trade or professional body**, where relevant to your work, and any registration details.

A clear footer and contact page usually cover these.

## Limited companies: extra details

Here's the second question. GOV.UK's guidance on [signs, stationery and promotional material](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material) says a limited company's website must show:

- The company's **registered name**.
- Its **registered number**.
- Its **registered office address**.
- **Where it's registered** (for example, England and Wales).

If you trade under a different name from your registered company name, you still need to show the registered details. The footer is the usual place.

## Sole traders and partnerships

If you trade under a name that isn't your own, there are rules about disclosing the owner's name and an address for documents. GOV.UK's guidance on [setting up as a sole trader](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-sole-trader) is a good starting point.

## Personal data and cookies

If your website has a contact form, booking tool or newsletter sign-up, you're collecting personal data. You need a privacy notice explaining what you collect and why. My guide to [privacy policies for small business websites](/blog/privacy-policy-small-business-website/) covers what to include.

Cookies and similar technologies have separate rules. My guide on [whether you need a cookie banner](/blog/do-i-need-a-cookie-banner/) explains them.

## Selling online

Here's the third question. If customers can buy goods, services or digital content through your website, more rules apply. GOV.UK's guidance on [online and distance selling](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) covers:

- **Information** you must give customers before they buy.
- **Cancellation rights** for many online purchases.
- **Delivery and refunds.**

This is where proper terms and conditions matter. They should be written for what you actually sell, not copied from another business.

## Do you need website terms and conditions?

If you **don't sell online**, many small business websites don't need a long "website terms of use" page. Focus on accurate business information, a privacy notice, and clear quotes or contracts for the work you do offline.

If you **do sell online**, you need terms covering those sales, written for your business. Consider professional legal advice.

## The aha: copied legal pages can be worse than none

It's tempting to copy a privacy policy or terms page from a bigger company. But it will describe their business, their suppliers, their returns policy and their data, not yours.

An inaccurate legal page can mislead customers and create commitments you didn't mean to make. A short, accurate page written for your business is far better.

## A quick checklist

1. Business name, address, email and phone clearly shown?
2. VAT number, if registered?
3. Registered company details in the footer, if a limited company?
4. Trade body membership or registration shown, where relevant?
5. A privacy notice covering your forms and tools?
6. Cookie rules checked?
7. Proper terms, if you sell online?
