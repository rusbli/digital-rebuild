"I just need a website."

It's how most website conversations start, and it's the start of a lot of misunderstandings. One person imagines three simple pages. Another imagines an online shop. The quote comes back and nobody's happy.

A short brief fixes that. It doesn't need to be long or technical. One page, in your own words, is enough to make quotes comparable and the finished website closer to what you pictured.

> **Quick answer:** A good website brief covers: what your business does, who your customers are, what the website should achieve (calls, bookings, sales), the pages you need, sites you like and dislike, your budget and deadline, and what content you already have. Write it in plain English, send the same brief to every designer, and you'll get quotes you can actually compare.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What should the brief cover?
- Why does "what should it achieve" matter most?
- Should I share my budget?

## The brief template

Here's the first question. Copy these headings and fill them in.

### 1. About your business

- What you do, in a sentence or two.
- Where you work.
- How long you've been trading.

### 2. Your customers

- Who they are: homeowners, landlords, businesses, families.
- What they usually need from you.
- How they find you now.

### 3. What the website should achieve

- More phone calls? Quote requests? Bookings? Online sales?
- What would make you say "that was worth it" a year from now?

### 4. Pages you think you need

A rough list: home, services (and which ones), about, contact, gallery, prices. The designer can suggest changes. My guide on [one-page websites versus several pages](/blog/one-page-website-or-multi-page/) can help.

### 5. Features

- Contact form, booking, online payments, WhatsApp button, reviews, blog.

### 6. Websites you like and dislike

Two or three of each, with a sentence on why. "I like how simple this is." "This one feels cluttered."

### 7. What you already have

- Logo and brand colours.
- Photos.
- Text.
- Existing website and domain, with logins.

### 8. Budget and timescale

- Your budget, or a range.
- When you'd like it live, and why (a launch, a busy season).

### 9. Who decides

- Who will give feedback and sign off?

## Start with the goal

Here's the second question. "What should the website achieve?" is the most important section, and the one most people skip.

A website built to get emergency callouts looks very different from one built to win big renovation projects, or to take bookings. The goal decides what goes at the top of each page, what the buttons say, and what success looks like.

So be specific. "More enquiries for kitchen fitting in Cardiff and Penarth" is far more useful than "a professional website".

## Should you share your budget?

Here's the third question. Yes.

Without a budget, designers have to guess. Some will quote high to cover every possibility; some will quote low and leave things out. With a budget, they can tell you what's realistic, or offer options.

If you're not sure what to budget, my guide to [what a small business website costs](/blog/small-business-website-cost-uk/) explains the typical costs to consider.

## The aha: the same brief makes quotes comparable

When you send different designers the same written brief, their quotes are suddenly comparable. You can see who's included what, who's suggested something extra, and who's misunderstood.

Without a brief, every designer works from a different conversation, and you end up comparing apples with oranges.

## Gather content while you brief

Your brief is also a good moment to start gathering content. Most website delays come from waiting for text and photos. My guide on [how long a website takes to build](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-build-a-website/) explains why.

## Questions to ask designers about your brief

Once you've sent it:

- What would you change or add, and why?
- What's included in your quote, and what isn't?
- What will I pay every year after launch?
- Who will own the domain, files and accounts?
- How long will it take, and what do you need from me?

My guide to [hiring a web designer](/blog/hiring-a-web-designer-cardiff/) covers these questions in more detail.

## A quick checklist

1. Business, customers and areas described?
2. A specific goal for the website?
3. A rough list of pages and features?
4. Examples of sites you like and dislike?
5. Budget and deadline?
6. Existing content and logins listed?
7. Who signs off?

One page is enough. It'll save hours of back-and-forth, and you'll be far more likely to get the website you pictured.
