You open Google Analytics. There are dozens of reports, hundreds of numbers and a sidebar full of menus.

You close it again and decide to look another day.

That's completely understandable. Google Analytics is built for everyone from corner shops to global brands, so it shows far more than a small business needs.

You only need a handful of numbers. Here they are, and what to do with each one.

> **Quick answer:** For most small businesses, the useful numbers in Google Analytics are: how many people visit, where they come from (Google, social media, directories, direct), which pages they land on, and, most importantly, how many get in touch. Set up tracking for form submissions and phone clicks so you can see enquiries, not just visits. Check monthly and look at trends over three months.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Which numbers actually matter?
- How do I see enquiries, not just visitors?
- What's the difference between Analytics and Search Console?

## Analytics and Search Console do different jobs

Here's the third question first, because it clears up a lot of confusion.

- **Google Search Console** shows what happens **before** someone visits: which Google searches showed your site and how many clicked. My [Search Console guide](/blog/google-search-console-for-small-business/) covers it.
- **Google Analytics** shows what happens **after** they arrive: where they came from, what they looked at, and whether they got in touch.

Together they tell you the whole story. Analytics on its own can't show you the searches people used.

## The numbers that matter

Here's the first question.

### 1. Users

How many people visited. Useful as a trend: is it growing, steady or dropping? Don't panic about week-to-week wobbles.

### 2. Where visitors come from

Analytics groups visits by source, usually something like:

- **Organic search**: from Google or other search engines, without ads.
- **Direct**: people who typed your address or used a bookmark.
- **Referral**: links from other websites, like directories.
- **Organic social**: from social media.
- **Paid**: from ads, if you run them.

This tells you which efforts are working. If you've put effort into your Google Business Profile or articles, you want organic search to grow.

### 3. Landing pages

Which pages people arrive on first. This shows you which pages are doing the work of bringing people in, and which pages deserve the most attention.

### 4. Enquiries (the one that matters most)

Visits are only useful if some of them become enquiries. That's what the next section is about.

## How to see enquiries, not just visitors

Here's the second question. Out of the box, Analytics tells you about visits. To see enquiries, you need to track the actions that count:

- **Contact form submissions.**
- **Clicks on your phone number.**
- **Clicks on your email address.**
- **Bookings**, if you have online booking.

In Google Analytics 4, these are tracked as **events**, and the important ones can be marked as **key events**. Setting this up properly usually needs a small change to your website, so ask your web designer if you're not comfortable doing it.

Once it's working, you can see things like: "Of the 300 people who visited last month, 12 got in touch, and 7 of them first landed on the boiler installation page."

That's the kind of insight you can act on.

## What you can safely ignore

- **Bounce rate and engagement rate**, on their own. They're easily misread.
- **Real-time reports.** Interesting, but not useful for decisions.
- **Demographics and interests.** Rarely actionable for a local business.
- **Day-to-day changes.** Small numbers swing wildly.

## The aha: low traffic isn't always the problem

When enquiries are low, people often assume they need more visitors.

Sometimes they do. But if Analytics shows a steady flow of visitors and very few enquiries, the problem is what happens on the website, not how many people arrive.

Getting twice as many visitors to a page that doesn't persuade anyone just gives you twice as many people leaving. My guide to [websites that get visitors but no enquiries](/blog/website-not-getting-enquiries/) covers what to fix first.

## A monthly ten-minute check

1. Compare users over the last three months with the three before.
2. Check which sources are growing or shrinking.
3. Look at the top landing pages.
4. Check how many enquiries (form submissions and phone clicks) you got, and from which pages.
5. Pick one page to improve based on what you've seen.

## Cookies and privacy

Analytics tools set cookies or similar technologies, so they come under UK rules on storage and access. Those rules changed in 2026 to add an exception for some statistical analytics, with conditions. Whether your set-up qualifies depends on how it's configured and how the data is used. My guide on [whether you need a cookie banner](/blog/do-i-need-a-cookie-banner/) explains the rules and links to the ICO's guidance.

## Setting it up

If you don't have Analytics yet:

1. Create an account at [Google Analytics](https://analytics.google.com/).
2. Add a property for your website.
3. Add the tracking code to your website, or ask your web designer to.
4. Set up events for form submissions and phone clicks.
5. Give it a few weeks to collect data before drawing conclusions.

And make sure you're the owner of the account, not just your designer. If you ever part ways, you'll want to keep your website's history.
