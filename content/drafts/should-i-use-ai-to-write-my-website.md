You can now ask an AI tool to "write a homepage for a plumber in Barry" and get something back in seconds. It'll be grammatically perfect, nicely structured, and full of phrases like "reliable, professional service you can trust".

So why pay anyone to write your website, or spend hours doing it yourself?

AI tools genuinely help. But there's a catch, and it's the same catch that makes most small business websites forgettable.

> **Quick answer:** AI tools are useful for getting started, organising ideas, editing and tidying your own words. But AI-drafted text is often generic, can include facts that aren't true, and sounds like every other business using the same tools. Use AI as an assistant, not the author: feed it your real details, check every fact, and rewrite anything that doesn't sound like you.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What does Google say about AI-written content?
- Where does AI genuinely help?
- How do I stop it sounding generic?

## What Google says

Here's the first question. Google's position, set out in its guidance on [creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content), is that it cares about content being helpful and reliable, not about how it's produced.

But its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) also describe **scaled content abuse**: producing lots of pages mainly to manipulate rankings rather than help people. That applies whether the pages are written by AI, by people or by both.

So AI isn't banned. Unhelpful, mass-produced content is the problem.

## Where AI genuinely helps

Here's the second question.

- **Getting past a blank page.** Ask for a rough structure for a services page.
- **Organising your notes.** Paste in your messy notes and ask it to put them in a sensible order.
- **Editing your own words.** Ask it to shorten a paragraph or make it clearer.
- **Generating questions.** "What questions would a homeowner ask before booking a rewire?" can spark useful FAQ ideas.
- **Checking tone.** Ask it to flag jargon or overly long sentences.

In all of these, you're providing the substance. The tool is helping you shape it.

## Where AI causes problems

- **Generic text.** Without your specific details, AI writes the average of every business like yours. "Quality workmanship", "competitive prices", "customer satisfaction".
- **Made-up facts.** AI tools can confidently state things that aren't true: a regulation, a price, a qualification you don't hold. Publishing those can mislead customers.
- **No real experience.** AI can't know how you actually work, what you're proud of, or what your customers say about you.
- **Sounding like everyone else.** If your competitors use the same tools with the same prompts, you'll end up with very similar websites.

## How to use it without sounding generic

Here's the third question.

### 1. Start with your own notes

Write down, in your own words:

- What you do and where.
- Who you help.
- How a job works, step by step.
- What you charge, or how you price.
- What customers often say about you.
- What you do differently.

### 2. Give the tool those notes

Ask it to turn your notes into a draft, without adding claims you haven't given it.

### 3. Check every fact

Prices, qualifications, regulations, timescales. If you didn't provide it, question it.

### 4. Rewrite in your voice

Read it aloud. If it sounds like a brochure, change it to how you'd say it to a customer. My guide to [describing your services in plain English](/blog/describing-your-services-in-plain-english/) has examples.

### 5. Add what only you know

Specific details make the difference: the area you cover, the way you protect floors, the reminder you send before a service is due.

## The aha: specific beats polished

AI is very good at polished. It's bad at specific. And customers choose businesses that sound specific and real.

"I'll text you a two-hour arrival window and let you know if I'm running late" beats any amount of polished "reliable service". You can only write that line because it's true for you.

Clear, specific text also helps AI search tools describe you accurately. My guide on [getting mentioned in AI search answers](/blog/get-recommended-by-ai-search/) explains why.

## If someone else writes your website

If you're paying a designer or copywriter, it's fair to ask how they write, and to give them your own notes. My guide on [how to brief a web designer](/blog/how-to-brief-a-web-designer/) includes what to share. Whoever writes it, the best websites are built on the owner's real knowledge.

## A quick checklist

1. Did I write the key facts and details myself?
2. Has every fact been checked?
3. Does it sound like me, read aloud?
4. Are there specific details only my business could say?
5. Have I removed generic phrases that any business could use?
