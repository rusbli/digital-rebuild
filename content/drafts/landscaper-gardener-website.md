Landscaping and gardening are about as visual as trades get. A customer can't picture a new patio, a raised bed or a reshaped lawn from a description. They need to see it.

That makes your website a portfolio first and a brochure second.

It also makes the job slightly different depending on what you do. Someone wanting a fortnightly gardener wants reliability. Someone planning a full garden transformation wants inspiration and reassurance. Your website needs to talk to both, if you offer both.

> **Quick answer:** A landscaping or gardening website should lead with before-and-after photos of real projects, with a short story for each. Separate regular maintenance from bigger landscaping projects, because they attract different customers. Explain how a project runs, give honest guidance on cost, show genuine reviews and the area you cover, and make it easy to book a visit, including for work later in the year.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Should maintenance and landscaping be on separate pages?
- What makes a project gallery persuasive?
- How do I use the website to fill quiet months?

## Two customers, two pages

Here's the first question. If you do both garden maintenance and landscaping projects, give them separate pages.

| Customer | What they want | What persuades them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regular maintenance | Reliability, tidiness, fair price | Clear schedule options, prices, reviews about reliability |
| Landscaping project | Inspiration, skill, trust with a big spend | Project galleries, process, design approach, cost guidance |

Mixing them makes both harder to find and harder to sell. My guide to [writing a services page that brings in enquiries](/blog/services-page-that-gets-enquiries/) explains the structure for each.

## Project pages that persuade

Here's the second question. A random gallery of garden photos is fine. Project pages are much better.

For each good project:

- **The brief**: what the customer wanted. "A low-maintenance garden with space for a table and a play area."
- **Before photos**, from fixed spots.
- **During photos**, showing groundwork and structure.
- **After photos**, from the same spots as the before shots.
- **What you did**: paving, fencing, turf, planting, drainage.
- **How long it took.**
- **Where**, by town or area.
- **A review** from the customer, if they're happy to give one.

Before-and-after pairs from the same angle are the most powerful photos you can have. My guide to [taking website photos with your phone](/blog/photos-for-your-website-with-a-phone/) has tips for getting them right.

Each project page also gives Google something specific to match: a "porcelain patio in Penarth" page can appear for exactly that kind of search.

## Explain how a project runs

Like a builder's customer, someone planning a big garden project worries about the unknowns. Explain:

1. **First visit**: you look at the space and discuss ideas.
2. **Design and quote**: what's included, how detailed it is.
3. **Scheduling**: how far ahead you're booking, and how the weather affects things.
4. **The work**: access, mess, protecting their property.
5. **Finish and aftercare**: what happens once it's done, and how to look after new planting or turf.

My guide to [what a builder's website needs](/blog/builder-website-what-to-include/) covers this kind of process page in more detail.

## Guidance on cost

- **Maintenance**: hourly or per-visit rates, minimum visits, what's included.
- **Projects**: what drives cost (materials, size, access, drainage), and typical ranges from your own recent work, if you're comfortable sharing them.
- **Whether quotes or design visits are free.**

Use your own real figures, and update them when prices change.

## Using your website to fill quiet months

Here's the third question. Gardening is seasonal, and winter can be quiet. Your website can help:

- **Encourage early booking.** "Planning a spring project? Book a design visit now."
- **Promote winter work**: hedge cutting, clearance, fencing, tree work, if you do them.
- **Offer maintenance contracts** that spread work across the year.
- **Show winter projects in your gallery**, so customers know work continues.

Winter is when many people plan their spring and summer projects. A website that makes it easy to book ahead captures them before they go elsewhere.

## The aha: show the garden a few months later

A newly finished garden can look a little bare: new turf, small plants, bright new paving.

If you can, go back a few months later (with permission) and photograph it again, once it's grown in. Those photos show customers what their garden will really look like, and they show you build gardens that last.

## Trust signals

- Genuine reviews.
- Insurance you hold.
- Any qualifications or memberships you genuinely have.
- Your van, team and equipment in photos.
- The area you cover, listed clearly.

## A quick checklist

1. Maintenance and landscaping on separate pages, if you offer both?
2. Project pages with before, during and after photos?
3. A clear explanation of how a project runs?
4. Honest guidance on cost?
5. Seasonal messages encouraging people to book ahead?
6. Area, reviews and an easy way to book a visit?
