Here's a story that plays out more often than it should.

A business owner falls out with their web designer, or the designer retires, or simply stops answering emails. The owner decides to move to someone new.

Then they discover the domain name, the address on their van and business cards, is registered to the designer. The website files are on the designer's server. The Google account that holds their analytics belongs to the designer too.

They've been paying for years. But they don't own it.

The good news: you can check where you stand today, in about fifteen minutes, before anything goes wrong.

> **Quick answer:** Your website is really several separate things: the domain name, the hosting, the website files, and the accounts connected to it, such as Google Business Profile and Analytics. Check who owns each one. The most important is the domain. It should be registered in your name, in an account you can log in to. If it isn't, ask for it to be transferred now, while relations are good.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Which parts of a website can you actually own?
- How do you check, without being technical?
- What do you do if it turns out you don't own it?

## Your website is really four things

Here's the first question. When people say "my website", they usually mean four separate things, and each can be owned by a different person.

| Part | What it is | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domain name | Your address, such as yourbusiness.co.uk | It's on your vans, cards and emails. Lose it and customers can't find you. |
| Hosting | The server your website lives on | Whoever controls it controls whether your site is online. |
| Website files | The design, code, text and images | Without them, moving means starting again. |
| Connected accounts | Google Business Profile, Search Console, Analytics, email | They hold your reviews, data and history. |

Owning one doesn't mean you own the others. It's quite possible to own your domain but have your website built on a platform you can't take with you, or to own the files but have your email set up through someone else's account.

## The domain matters most

If you only check one thing, check this.

Your domain is the part customers know. It's printed everywhere. If you lose control of it, you lose your email address too, if your email uses it.

A domain is registered through a company called a registrar, in the name of a registrant. **The registrant is the legal holder.** That's the name you want to be yours.

### How to check

1. **Find out who the registrar is.** If you set it up yourself, look for old emails about renewal or registration. If someone else set it up, ask them.
2. **Log in to the registrar account**, if you have one. Look at the domain's registrant or owner details. Is it your name or your business's name?
3. **If you don't have a login**, ask whoever set it up: "Which registrar is my domain with, whose name is it registered in, and can I have access to the account?"

You can also try a public WHOIS lookup, which shows some details about a domain. But for privacy reasons the owner's name is often hidden, so your registrar account is the reliable check.

> **The quick test:** can you log in to the place your domain is registered, and does it show your name as the owner? If both answers are yes, you're in good shape. If either is no, that's your first job.

## Then check the rest

### Hosting

Ask: "Where is my website hosted, and is the hosting account in my name?"

It's quite common, and fine, for a designer to host sites for clients as part of a care plan. What matters is that you know where it is, and that the designer will hand over the files if you leave.

### Website files

If your site is custom-built, ask: "If I wanted to move my website to another host, would you send me the files?"

If your site is on a builder like Wix or Squarespace, the answer is different. The site is built in their system and generally can't be moved elsewhere as it is. My guide comparing [Wix, Squarespace and custom websites](/blog/wix-squarespace-or-custom-website/) explains that trade-off.

### Connected accounts

These are easy to forget, and they hold a lot of value:

- **Google Business Profile.** You should be the owner, not just a manager added by someone else. Your reviews live here.
- **Google Search Console and Google Analytics.** Ask to be made an owner or administrator. Your website's history and data live here.
- **Email.** If your email uses your domain, check who controls the email account settings.
- **Social media pages.** Make sure you're an admin, not just the designer.

## Why this happens (it's usually not sinister)

It's tempting to assume the worst, but most ownership problems aren't deliberate.

Often a designer simply registers the domain in their own account because it's quicker, intending to sort it later. Or a family member set things up years ago with their own email. Or the person who built the site has since moved on, and nobody wrote down the logins.

That's why it's worth checking while everyone's on good terms. A friendly "Can we make sure the domain's in my name?" is easy to sort out today and much harder after a disagreement.

## What to do if you don't own it

Here's the second and third questions together. If you've found something in someone else's name:

1. **Ask politely, in writing.** Email the person or company and ask for the domain to be transferred into your name, and for access to the other accounts. Most people will help.
2. **Be specific.** List each item: the domain, hosting access or files, Google Business Profile ownership, Analytics and Search Console access.
3. **Don't change anything else first.** Moving hosts or cancelling payments before you have control can take your site or email offline.
4. **Get help if you're stuck.** If someone refuses, or you can't reach them, contact the registrar. Registrars have their own processes for disputes, and the outcome often depends on evidence that the domain is genuinely yours, such as invoices and business records.

## Make it part of any new website deal

If you're getting a new website built, settle ownership before you start. Ask:

- Will the domain be registered in my name, in an account I control?
- Will I get the website files if I ever leave?
- Will I be the owner of my Google Business Profile, Search Console and Analytics?
- What happens to the site if I stop paying for ongoing support?

Good designers will answer all four easily. I make sure every client owns their domain, code and content from day one, because it shouldn't be up for debate.

These questions sit alongside the cost questions in my guide to [what a small business website costs in the UK](/blog/small-business-website-cost-uk/). Ownership is part of the price, even if it isn't written on the quote.

## Your fifteen-minute checklist

1. Find out which registrar your domain is with.
2. Log in, or get a login, and check the registrant is you.
3. Find out where your site is hosted and how you'd get the files.
4. Check you're the owner of your Google Business Profile.
5. Check you have owner access to Search Console and Analytics.
6. Write all of this down, with the logins stored safely in a password manager.

Do it once, and you'll never face the "who owns my website?" surprise.
