Think about why someone visits a café or restaurant website.

They're not there to read your story. Not yet. They want to know whether you're open, where you are, what you serve and whether they can book.

That's it. Four questions, usually on a phone, often while standing on the pavement deciding where to eat.

If your website answers those four instantly, you'll win visits that other places lose. Then the rest of the site can do the persuading.

> **Quick answer:** A café or restaurant website should make four things instant on a phone: opening hours, location (with a map and parking information), a current menu with prices, and a way to book or get in touch. Put the menu on a normal web page rather than a PDF. Then add real photos, dietary information, your food hygiene rating and what makes you different.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What are the four things, and where should they go?
- Why is a PDF menu a problem?
- What makes someone choose you over the place next door?

## The four things, on the first screen

Here's the first question.

### 1. Opening hours

Exactly as they are today, including any seasonal changes. Wrong opening hours are one of the fastest ways to lose a customer's trust. If you close for holidays, update the site the same day.

Keep your hours identical on your website and your Google Business Profile.

### 2. Location

- Your address, with a map.
- Parking: where to park, and whether it's free.
- Public transport, if relevant.
- Accessibility: step-free access, accessible toilets.

### 3. Menu and prices

A current menu, with prices. More on the format below.

### 4. How to book or get in touch

A booking button if you take reservations, or a phone number people can tap. If you don't take bookings, say so clearly: "Walk-ins only, no bookings."

On a phone, all four should be reachable within one tap from the top of the page.

## The problem with PDF menus

Here's the second question. Lots of cafés and restaurants upload their menu as a PDF. It's easy, but it causes problems:

- **Slow on mobile**, especially on a weak signal.
- **Hard to read**: pinching and zooming on a tiny phone screen.
- **Hard for Google to use**, compared with normal web page text.
- **Easily out of date**, because updating means redesigning the PDF.

A menu written as a normal web page is faster, easier to read and easier to update. You can still offer a printable version for people who want one.

## What makes someone choose you

Here's the third question. Once the basics are covered, this is where you persuade.

### Photos of your real food and space

Not stock photos. Good, well-lit photos of your actual dishes, your counter and your room tell people exactly what to expect. A phone camera in good daylight is enough.

### What makes you different

One or two honest sentences. Locally sourced, homemade cakes, dog-friendly, great for families, the best view in Penarth. Whatever is genuinely true.

### Dietary information

Mark vegetarian, vegan and gluten-free options clearly, and explain how customers can ask about allergens. Many groups choose where to go based on one person's dietary needs.

### Your food hygiene rating

In Wales, food businesses have to display their food hygiene rating at their premises. Showing it on your website too is a good way to build trust, and customers can check any business on the [Food Standards Agency's ratings website](https://ratings.food.gov.uk/).

### Reviews

A few genuine reviews, with a link to your Google reviews. My guide to [showing up in near-me searches](/blog/how-to-show-up-in-near-me-searches/) explains why reviews matter for being found as well as chosen.

## Bookings and orders

If you take table bookings, a booking tool linked from your site can save phone calls, especially in the evening when you're busy. My guide to [online booking for small businesses](/blog/online-booking-for-small-business/) covers what to check before choosing one.

If you do takeaway or delivery, link clearly to how people order.

## The aha: your Google listing is your real front door

For cafés and restaurants, many customers never visit your website at all. They see your Google Business Profile in the map results, check the hours, photos and reviews, and turn up.

So keep your profile just as current as your website: hours, photos, menu link and special opening times. And when someone does click through to your website, it should confirm and expand on what they've just seen, not contradict it.

## Speed matters more than design

People checking a café website are often outside, on mobile data, in a hurry. A slow site loses them. Big videos, huge images and heavy animations look good on a desktop but cost you on a phone.

My guide on [how fast a website should load](/blog/how-fast-should-a-website-load/) explains how to test yours.

## A quick checklist

1. Opening hours, location, menu and booking all within one tap on a phone?
2. Hours identical on your website and Google profile?
3. Menu on a normal web page, with prices?
4. Real photos of your food and space?
5. Dietary information and food hygiene rating shown?
6. A fast-loading page, even on mobile data?
