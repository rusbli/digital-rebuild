It always arrives at the worst moment. You're between jobs, you check your phone, and there it is: one star, and a paragraph that makes your stomach drop.

Maybe it's fair. Maybe it's wildly unfair. Maybe you don't even recognise the name.

Your first instinct will be to reply straight away and set the record straight. Don't. Not yet.

Here's how to handle it so that, a month from now, that review is doing you more good than harm.

> **Quick answer:** Wait until you're calm. Then reply briefly and politely: thank them, acknowledge their experience, explain in a sentence without arguing, and offer to sort it out directly. Never share private details or get into a back-and-forth. If the review breaks Google's rules, for example it's fake or from someone who was never a customer, report it through your Business Profile.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Who is my reply really for?
- What do I say when the review is unfair?
- When can I get a review removed?

## Your reply isn't for the reviewer

Here's the first question, and it changes everything.

The person who left the review has probably made up their mind. Your reply is unlikely to change it.

The people you're really writing for are the **future customers** who'll read that review, and your reply, while deciding whether to call you.

They're not looking for a perfect record. They're looking at how you behave when things go wrong. A calm, reasonable reply tells them you're the kind of business that sorts problems out. An angry or defensive one tells them the opposite, and it'll stay there for years.

## The four-part reply

For most negative reviews, this structure works:

1. **Thank them** for taking the time.
2. **Acknowledge** their experience, without necessarily agreeing with every detail.
3. **Explain briefly**, if there's something useful to add. One or two sentences. No essays.
4. **Offer to put it right** directly, with a way to contact you.

For example:

> Thanks for your feedback, Sarah. I'm sorry the job didn't go the way you expected. We arrived later than planned because of an earlier emergency callout, and I should have phoned you sooner to let you know. I'd like to put this right, so please call me directly on 07... and we'll sort it out. Dan

Short, human and constructive. A reader comes away thinking "fair enough".

## When the review is unfair

Here's the second question. Sometimes the review is simply wrong. The price was agreed in writing. The delay was their choice. The problem was caused by something you'd warned them about.

It's tempting to prove it. Resist.

Instead:

- **State the facts once, calmly.** "Our written quote included the extra work on the boiler, which was agreed before we started."
- **Don't accuse.** Avoid "you're lying" or "that's not what happened".
- **Don't share private details**, like their address, what they paid or personal circumstances.
- **Still offer to talk.** It shows you're reasonable even when you disagree.

Readers are good at spotting an unreasonable review when the reply beneath it is calm. You don't need to win the argument in public.

## Replies that backfire

- **Arguing line by line.** It looks petty, however right you are.
- **Sarcasm.** It reads badly to every future customer.
- **Revealing private information.** Never mention someone's address, payment details or personal life.
- **Copy-and-paste replies.** "Sorry for your experience, please contact us" on every review looks robotic.
- **Offering something in exchange for removing the review.** Google's policy prohibits offering incentives for changing or removing a review.

## When you can report a review

Here's the third question. Google won't remove a review just because it's negative or you think it's unfair. It may remove reviews that break its rules.

Google's [policy on prohibited content](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) covers fake engagement, which includes content not based on a real experience. Reviews may also break other content policies, such as offensive language or conflicts of interest.

Situations worth reporting:

- The reviewer was **never a customer**, and you can't find any record of them.
- The review is clearly from a **competitor** or someone with a conflict of interest.
- It contains **offensive, hateful or personal content**.
- It's about a **different business** with a similar name.

Report it through your Google Business Profile. Google decides whether it breaks the rules. In the meantime, it's still worth posting a calm reply, so readers see your side.

## The aha: one bad review can help

A profile with nothing but five-star reviews can look too good to be true. A profile with a few honest mixed reviews, and thoughtful replies, often looks more believable.

Customers don't expect perfection. They expect a business that takes problems seriously. Your reply to a bad review is one of the clearest places to show that.

## Learn something from it

Once the sting has worn off, ask yourself honestly:

- Is there a grain of truth in it?
- Did something go wrong in how we communicated?
- Is there a small change that would stop it happening again?

Often the most useful complaints are the ones about communication, not workmanship. A quick text when you're running late prevents a surprising number of one-star reviews.

## A simple routine

1. Read the review. Close the app.
2. Check the facts: job records, messages, quotes.
3. Draft a reply using the four parts. Wait an hour, then reread it.
4. Post it.
5. Report it if it breaks Google's rules.
6. Note anything you'll do differently.
