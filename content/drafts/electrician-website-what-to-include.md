Nobody browses electricians for fun.

People find you because something's gone wrong (the lights keep tripping, a socket's scorched) or because they're planning something bigger, like a rewire, an extension or an EV charger. Either way, they're a bit anxious. Electricity is the one trade most homeowners know they can't bodge.

That anxiety is the key to an electrician's website. The job isn't to look impressive. It's to make a nervous person feel safe enough to ring you instead of the next name on Google.

> **Quick answer:** An electrician's website should say what you do and where at the top, with a tappable phone number. Give each main service its own page. Show your competent person scheme registration if you have one, so customers can check it. Add real photos, genuine reviews and an honest idea of price. Make the contact form short and test it.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What makes a homeowner trust one electrician over another online?
- Which services deserve their own page?
- How much should you say about prices?

## Start with the panicking visitor

Someone whose kitchen sockets have just died wants three answers fast:

1. Do you do this kind of work?
2. Do you cover my area?
3. How do I reach you now?

So the top of your home page, before anyone scrolls, needs a plain headline such as "Electrician in Barry and the Vale of Glamorgan", one line on what you cover, and a big phone number they can tap.

If you offer same-day callouts, say so, and say when. "Same-day fault finding, Monday to Saturday" is honest. "24/7 emergency service" is only worth writing if you'll genuinely answer at 2am.

## Registration is your strongest trust signal

Here's the first question. For electricians, one thing does more than any amount of nice design: proof that you're registered.

In England and Wales, electrical work in homes falls under Part P of the Building Regulations. Electricians registered with a government-authorised **competent person scheme** can self-certify their notifiable work. GOV.UK's page on [using a competent person scheme](https://www.gov.uk/building-regulations-approval/use-a-competent-person-scheme) explains how this works, and homeowners can check an electrician on the [Electrical Competent Person Register](https://www.electricalcompetentperson.co.uk/).

So if you're registered:

- Show the scheme logo you're entitled to use.
- Show your registration details, or say clearly how customers can check you.
- Link to the register so they can verify it themselves.

A homeowner who checks and finds your name is a homeowner who's ready to book.

Never display a logo or registration you don't hold. Apart from being dishonest, it's the quickest way to lose a customer who checks.

## Give your main services their own pages

Here's the second question. Most electricians do a wide range of work, but a few services bring in most of the money. Those deserve their own pages.

Typical candidates:

| Service | What that customer wants to know |
| --- | --- |
| Fault finding and repairs | Can you come soon? What's the call-out charge? |
| Consumer unit upgrades | Why do I need one? How long will the power be off? |
| Full or partial rewires | How disruptive is it? How long? Rough cost? |
| EV charger installation | Will it work with my car and driveway? What's involved? |
| Electrical condition reports | Landlords and buyers: how quickly can you do it, what does it cost? |
| Lighting and extensions | Can I see examples of your work? |

A separate page lets you answer each customer's real questions. It also gives Google a page to match against a specific search, which a single "Services" list can't do.

You don't need all of these. Write pages for the work you want more of, and leave out the jobs you'd rather not do.

## Prices: say something rather than nothing

Here's the third question. Every electrician says "every job's different". It's true, and it's also what every customer hears just before they click away to someone who gave them a number.

You don't need a price list. Options that work:

- **A call-out or first-hour charge** for fault finding.
- **Starting prices** for standard jobs, such as an electrical condition report for a typical house.
- **Typical ranges** for big jobs: "Most three-bedroom rewires we quote fall between £X and £Y."
- **An explanation of how you quote**, if you genuinely can't give numbers: do you visit, is the visit free, how fast do quotes arrive?

Use your own real figures and review them every few months.

## Show the work, not stock photos

A photo of a tidy consumer unit you installed says more than any sentence about quality.

Good photos for an electrician's site:

- Neat consumer units and wiring (before and after, if you can).
- Finished lighting schemes.
- Your van, with your name on it.
- You, at work.

Ask customers before photographing their homes, and avoid anything showing house numbers or personal details.

## Reviews, accreditations and a real person

Round out the trust section with:

- **Genuine Google reviews**, linked or quoted with permission. My guide on [getting more Google reviews the honest way](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/) covers how to ask.
- **Insurance**, if you carry public liability cover.
- **Guarantees**, if you offer them.
- **A short paragraph about you**: how long you've been working and where you're based.

## The contact page and form

Keep the form short: name, phone, postcode and "What do you need doing?". A postcode tells you instantly if the job's in your area.

Add a line saying when you'll reply. And send yourself a test enquiry every month, because forms break silently.

## Don't forget your Google Business Profile

For local electricians, a lot of calls come straight from the map results without anyone visiting your website. Your profile and your website should match: same name, same number, same area.

If you haven't set it up properly, my [Google Business Profile guide for tradespeople](/blog/google-business-profile-for-trades/) covers it, including keeping your home address private.

## What to leave off

- Long histories on the home page.
- Logos you're not entitled to use.
- Technical jargon. "Consumer unit" might need a few words of explanation ("the fuse box") for many homeowners.
- A news page you won't update.

## A quick checklist

1. Can someone tell what you do, where and how to call within five seconds on a phone?
2. Does each main service have its own page?
3. Is your scheme registration visible and checkable?
4. Are there real photos and genuine reviews?
5. Is there at least a rough idea of price?
6. Does the form ask for a postcode, and does it actually send?
