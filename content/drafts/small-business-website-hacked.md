It often starts with a message from a customer. "Your website's showing something odd." Or you search for your business and see strange pages listed, in another language, selling things you've never heard of.

Your website has been hacked.

It's stressful, and it can feel personal. Usually it isn't. Most small business sites are hacked by automated tools looking for out-of-date software or weak passwords, not by anyone targeting you.

Here's what to do, in order.

> **Quick answer:** Don't panic, and don't delete everything. Contact your web designer or host, change all passwords (hosting, website admin, email and domain), and turn on two-step verification. Then clean the site, ideally by restoring a known clean backup and fixing whatever let the attacker in, usually out-of-date software or a weak password. Check Google Search Console's Security Issues report and request a review once clean.

Three questions we'll answer:

- How do I know it's really been hacked?
- What do I do in the first hour?
- How do I stop it happening again?

## Signs your site has been hacked

Here's the first question. Common signs:

- Pages, links or text you didn't add, often spam.
- Visitors being redirected to other websites.
- A "This site may be hacked" note in Google results.
- Strange results when you search `site:yourbusiness.co.uk`.
- New admin users you don't recognise.
- A warning from your host, Google Search Console or your browser.
- Your site suddenly running very slowly.

Hacked content can sometimes be hidden from you but shown to Google or other visitors, so search results and Search Console are worth checking even if the site looks normal to you.

## The first hour

Here's the second question.

### 1. Tell whoever looks after your site

Your web designer or host should be your first call. They may have backups and logs that help.

### 2. Change your passwords

Change every password connected to your website:

- Hosting account.
- Website admin logins.
- Domain registrar account.
- Email accounts.
- Any FTP or database accounts.

Use strong, unique passwords, and turn on two-step verification wherever it's offered. The National Cyber Security Centre's [Small Business Guide](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/small-business-guide) has practical advice on passwords and protecting accounts.

### 3. Don't delete everything in a panic

Deleting the site can destroy evidence of how the attacker got in, and make recovery harder. Let your designer or host advise.

## Cleaning up properly

- **Restore a clean backup**, from before the hack, if one exists.
- **Find and fix the way in.** Common causes include out-of-date software, plugins or themes, weak or reused passwords, and unused admin accounts. Google's [FAQ for hacked sites](https://web.dev/articles/faq-for-hacked-sites) lists these and other common causes.
- **Update everything** that can be updated.
- **Remove what you don't use**: old plugins, themes and admin users.
- **Check for hidden content and redirects** after the clean-up.

Restoring a backup without fixing the cause often leads to the same thing happening again.

## Dealing with Google

If Google has flagged your site:

1. Make sure the site is verified in Google Search Console.
2. Open the **Security Issues** report, which shows what Google found.
3. Once the whole site is clean and the cause fixed, request a review from that report.

Google's help page on [the 'This site may be hacked' message](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/190597) explains the process.

## How to stop it happening again

Here's the third question.

1. **Keep software updated.** For WordPress sites, that means WordPress, themes and plugins. My guide to [WordPress for small businesses](/blog/wordpress-for-small-business/) covers what's involved.
2. **Use strong, unique passwords** and two-step verification.
3. **Remove unused plugins, themes and users.**
4. **Keep regular backups**, stored separately, and test that they restore.
5. **Use HTTPS.** My guide to [fixing 'Not secure'](/blog/website-says-not-secure/) explains how.
6. **Decide who's responsible** for updates and security. If it's someone else, my guide to [website care plans](/blog/website-care-plan-cost/) explains what to expect.

## The aha: less software, less risk

Every plugin, theme and add-on is another thing that needs updating, and another possible way in.

Lean websites, with only what you actually use, have far less to go wrong. For a small business brochure site, simpler is often both faster and safer.

## Don't forget your email

If your email account was compromised too, check for forwarding rules or filters you didn't create, and warn customers if suspicious emails were sent from your address. Email security matters as much as website security, because your email can be used to reset your other passwords.

## A quick checklist

1. Designer or host contacted?
2. All passwords changed, with two-step verification on?
3. Clean backup restored, and the way in fixed?
4. Software updated and unused extras removed?
5. Google Search Console checked, and review requested if flagged?
6. A plan in place for updates, backups and security?
