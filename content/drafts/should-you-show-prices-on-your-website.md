You've probably had this argument with yourself.

Put prices on the website and you might scare people off, or let competitors undercut you. Leave them off and you get calls from people who hang up the moment they hear a number.

Most small businesses settle on "contact us for a quote" because it feels safer. I think that's usually the wrong call, and here's why, along with how to do it when your prices genuinely vary.

> **Quick answer:** For most small businesses, showing at least some pricing helps. It brings in enquiries from people who can afford you and filters out those who can't. If every job is different, show starting prices, typical ranges or a clear explanation of how you quote. Avoid fake precision, hidden extras and prices you haven't updated in years.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Does showing prices really put people off?
- What if every job I do is different?
- Won't competitors just undercut me?

## What "contact us for a price" actually tells a visitor

Picture someone comparing three local businesses on their phone in the evening.

One shows "Boiler service from £X". One shows "Prices from £X to £Y, depending on size". One says "Contact us for a quote".

The first two have answered the question they came with. The third has given them homework: write a message, wait for a reply, maybe have a phone call they don't feel like having.

Many people simply don't do the homework. They contact the businesses that gave them a number.

So here's the first question. Showing prices does put some people off: the ones who were never going to pay what you charge. Hiding prices puts off a different group: people who would have paid, but couldn't be bothered to ask. The second group is usually bigger, and it's the one you want.

## The aha: prices are a filter, not a barrier

Every quote you write takes time. Every site visit costs fuel and an hour or two.

When prices are hidden, you spend that time on everyone, including people who'll drop out the moment they hear the figure. When prices are visible, the people who contact you have already accepted the rough cost. Fewer enquiries, perhaps, but far more of them turn into work.

That's why adding prices can mean slightly fewer enquiries but the same number of bookings, or more. The enquiries that disappear are often the ones that never went anywhere.

## How to show prices when every job is different

Here's the second question. "Every job's different" is true for most trades and services. You still have options.

### Starting prices

"Bathroom refits from £X." "Logo design from £X." Honest, simple, and it sets expectations. Make sure the starting price is genuinely achievable for a small, straightforward job.

### Typical ranges

"Most kitchen fitting jobs we do cost between £X and £Y, depending on size and finish." Ranges feel more honest than a single "from" price, because people know the real cost depends on the details.

### Example jobs

Describe two or three typical jobs and what they cost: "A small en-suite with a new shower, toilet and basin, fully tiled: around £X." These help people place their own job in context.

### A clear explanation of how you quote

If numbers truly aren't possible, explain the process: "I visit, measure up and send a fixed written quote within two working days. The visit is free." That still removes uncertainty, which is most of what people want.

## What to put next to the prices

A bare number raises questions. Answer them beside it:

- **What's included**, and what isn't.
- **What changes the price**, such as size, materials, access, or urgency.
- **Whether VAT is included**, if you're VAT registered.
- **When the price was last updated**, if prices in your trade move a lot.

That context makes your price feel fair rather than arbitrary.

## Won't competitors undercut me?

Here's the third question. A competitor who wants your prices can get them in five minutes by asking you for a quote. Hiding them from your website doesn't stop that.

What hiding prices does stop is the customer who doesn't want to ask.

And most customers aren't choosing on price alone. They're weighing trust, reviews, how quickly you respond and whether you seem to know what you're doing. A clear price next to good reviews and real photos of your work often beats a cheaper competitor with a vague website.

## When not showing prices makes sense

There are fair exceptions:

- **Truly bespoke, high-value work**, where the range would be so wide it's meaningless.
- **Prices that change week to week** because of material costs, where any figure would be out of date quickly.
- **Work where you must assess safety first** before you can say anything sensible.

Even then, explain how you price and how quickly you'll respond. That's still better than "contact us".

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Prices you can't honour.** A "from" price that nobody ever actually pays damages trust.
- **Hidden extras.** If call-out fees, travel or disposal are extra, say so.
- **Out-of-date prices.** Put a reminder in your diary to check them every few months.
- **Too much small print.** If your pricing needs ten caveats, simplify it.

## Where prices belong on your website

- A short summary or starting prices on your home page, near your services.
- Fuller detail on each service page.
- A dedicated pricing page if you have packages.

For what it's worth, I show my own [website prices](/#pricing) openly, with what each package includes, because it's what I'd want as a customer. My guide to [what a small business website costs](/blog/small-business-website-cost-uk/) explains the thinking.

## Try it and measure

If you're unsure, run a simple test:

1. Note how many enquiries you get each month, and how many turn into paid work.
2. Add starting prices or ranges to your main service pages.
3. Compare after two or three months.

Look at bookings, not just enquiries. If enquiries dip but bookings hold or rise, the prices are doing their job. And if enquiries are low whatever you do, my guide to [websites that get visitors but no enquiries](/blog/website-not-getting-enquiries/) covers the other things to check.
