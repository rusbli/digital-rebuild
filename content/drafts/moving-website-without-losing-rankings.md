You're getting a new website. Maybe a new designer, a new platform, or even a new domain name.

It's exciting. But there's a risk most people don't hear about until it's too late: a careless move can lose the visitors and Google positions your old site built up over years.

The good news is that it's very avoidable. It just needs planning before the new site goes live.

> **Quick answer:** Before moving, list every page on your old site and note which bring in visitors. Keep the same addresses where you can; where they change, set up permanent (301) redirects from each old address to its closest new equivalent. Keep the content that works, make sure the new site is indexable and fast, then check Google Search Console closely in the weeks after launch.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What actually causes rankings to drop after a move?
- What should I do before launch?
- What should I check afterwards?

## What causes the drop

Here's the first question. Rankings usually fall after a rebuild for a few predictable reasons:

- **Page addresses change** and old ones lead nowhere. Anyone (or anything) following an old link gets an error, and Google loses track of what it knew about those pages.
- **Useful content is removed** or cut down in the redesign.
- **The new site blocks Google** by mistake, often a setting left on from the test version.
- **The new site is slower** or harder to use on mobile.
- **Titles and headings change** and no longer match what people search for.

All of these are preventable.

## Before launch

Here's the second question.

### 1. List every page on the old site

Write down every page address. Your old sitemap (often `yoursite.co.uk/sitemap.xml`) is a good starting point.

### 2. Find the pages that matter

In Google Search Console, the Performance report shows which pages get clicks from Google. My [Search Console guide](/blog/google-search-console-for-small-business/) explains where to look. Those pages need the most care.

### 3. Keep addresses where possible

If `/boiler-servicing/` works, keep it as `/boiler-servicing/` on the new site.

### 4. Redirect the ones that change

Where an address has to change, set up a **permanent (301) redirect** from the old address to the closest new page. Google's guidance on [site moves with URL changes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) recommends this approach.

Don't redirect everything to the home page. Send each old page to its closest match.

### 5. Keep what works

If a page brings in visitors, keep its useful content. Improve it, by all means, but don't throw it away for a shorter, prettier version.

### 6. Check the new site can be indexed

Make sure any "hide from search engines" setting from the test site is switched off.

## Moving to a new domain

If you're changing domain name as well, there's more to do:

- Redirect every old page to its equivalent on the new domain.
- Keep the old domain registered, and the redirects in place, for a long time.
- Update your Google Business Profile, directory listings and social profiles.
- Use the tools in Google Search Console for the move, as described in Google's guidance.

If you can avoid changing domain, it's simpler. If the reason is that you don't control your old domain, my guide on [what to do if your web designer has disappeared](/blog/web-designer-disappeared/) explains how to try to recover it first.

## After launch

Here's the third question. In the weeks after launch:

1. **Submit the new sitemap** in Search Console.
2. **Test old addresses**: type a few into your browser and check they redirect properly.
3. **Watch the Pages report** for new errors.
4. **Watch Performance** for drops in clicks to key pages.
5. **Fix problems quickly.**

Some fluctuation is normal while Google processes the changes. Big, lasting drops usually point to a missed redirect or a missing page.

## The aha: the old site's map is the most valuable thing you have

When people plan a new website, they focus on the new design. But the most important document in a rebuild is a boring list: every old page address, and where it should go on the new site.

Make that list before anything else. It takes an hour or two, and it protects years of work.

## When a rebuild is the right call

None of this should put you off a rebuild that's needed. A careful move to a faster, clearer site can improve your visibility. My guide on [whether your website needs a rebuild](/blog/signs-your-website-needs-a-rebuild/) helps you decide whether it's worth it.

## A quick checklist

1. Every old page listed, with the important ones flagged?
2. Addresses kept, or permanently redirected to their closest match?
3. Useful content kept?
4. New site indexable, fast and mobile-friendly?
5. Sitemap submitted and redirects tested?
6. Search Console checked weekly for the first month?
