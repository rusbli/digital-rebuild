"It's £X a month for maintenance."

Lots of small business owners hear this from their web designer, nod, and start paying. A year later, they're not quite sure what they're paying for.

Sometimes a care plan is excellent value. Sometimes it's money for very little. And sometimes it's a quiet way of keeping you locked in.

Here's how to tell which, and what a good plan should include.

> **Quick answer:** A website care plan usually covers updates, security, backups, monitoring and a set amount of small changes each month. Whether you need one depends on how your site is built: sites with lots of plugins and software need regular updates; lean, simple sites need far less. Before paying, check exactly what's included, how quickly they respond, and what happens to your site if you cancel.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Does my website actually need regular maintenance?
- What should a care plan include for the money?
- What happens if I stop paying?

## Does your site actually need maintenance?

Here's the first question, and the answer depends on how your site is built.

**Sites with lots of moving parts** need regular care. A typical WordPress site has the WordPress software itself, a theme and plugins, all of which get updates. Skipping updates for months can leave security holes or break things when you finally update.

**Simple, lean sites** need much less. A small hand-coded site with no plugins and no database has far fewer things to update or go wrong.

**Website builder sites** (Wix, Squarespace and similar) are maintained by the platform. You're already paying for that in your subscription. You may still want help with content changes, but not with software updates.

So before you agree to a plan, ask your designer: "What exactly needs maintaining on my site, and how often?"

## What a good care plan includes

Here's the second question. A decent plan usually covers:

| Item | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Software and security updates | Keeping everything up to date, and testing that nothing broke |
| Backups | Regular copies of the site, stored somewhere separate, that can actually be restored |
| Uptime monitoring | Someone gets an alert if your site goes down |
| Security monitoring | Watching for hacks, malware or suspicious activity |
| Small changes | A set amount of time each month for updates like new prices, photos or text |
| Support | Help when something goes wrong, with a stated response time |
| A simple report | What was done each month |

The most useful of these, in my view, is the small changes allowance. Keeping your prices, photos and services current is what keeps a website bringing in work.

## Questions to ask before signing up

- **What exactly is included?** Get a written list.
- **How much change time is included each month?** Does unused time roll over?
- **How quickly do you respond**, and to what?
- **How often are backups taken, where are they stored, and have you tested restoring one?**
- **Is hosting included?** If so, what happens to hosting if I cancel?
- **What's the notice period?**

## What happens if you stop paying?

Here's the third question, and it's the one that matters most.

Your site shouldn't vanish because you end a support plan. If the designer also hosts your site, you might need to move hosting, but they should help you do that and hand over the files.

Be cautious if:

- The domain is registered in their name, not yours.
- You don't have access to your site or hosting.
- They can't explain how you'd leave.

My guide on [who owns your website](/blog/who-owns-my-website/) explains how to check, and what to ask for.

## How to judge whether it's worth it

Think about what the plan replaces:

- **Your time**, if you'd otherwise do updates and changes yourself.
- **The risk** of a hacked or broken site, and the cost of fixing it in a hurry.
- **Pay-as-you-go fees** for small changes.

If you need regular changes, a plan can be cheaper than paying for each one. If your site rarely changes and needs little technical upkeep, pay-as-you-go may make more sense.

Prices vary widely, so compare what's included rather than just the monthly figure. My guide to [what a small business website costs](/blog/small-business-website-cost-uk/) explains how to compare ongoing costs over three years.

## The aha: a lean website needs less looking after

Many care plans exist because the website needs constant attention. The more plugins, scripts and add-ons a site has, the more updating it needs, and the more there is to go wrong.

A simpler site, with only what you actually use, needs less maintenance, and it's usually faster too. My guide on [how fast a website should load](/blog/how-fast-should-a-website-load/) touches on the same point.

So when you're choosing a website, it's worth asking not only "what does it cost to build?" but "how much looking after will it need?"

## A quick checklist

1. Does my site genuinely need regular updates?
2. Is there a written list of what's included?
3. Is there a set amount of change time each month?
4. Are backups tested, and stored separately?
5. Is the response time clear?
6. Do I own my domain and files, and can I leave easily?
