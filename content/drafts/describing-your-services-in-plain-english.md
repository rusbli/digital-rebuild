Read a few trade websites in a row and you'll notice something odd. They all say the same thing.

"Quality workmanship at competitive prices."

"No job too small."

"Bespoke solutions tailored to your needs."

"Your satisfaction is our priority."

None of it is wrong. None of it helps. A customer reading those lines learns nothing about what you actually do, or why they should pick you.

The fix isn't fancier writing. It's plainer writing.

> **Quick answer:** Describe your services the way you'd explain them to a customer on the phone: short sentences, everyday words, specific details. Say what you do, where, for whom, how it works and what it costs. Use the words customers search for, adding trade terms in brackets where useful. Replace every empty claim with a specific fact.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Why do slogans and jargon fail?
- How do I write if I'm not a writer?
- What does a good rewrite look like?

## Why slogans and jargon fail

Here's the first question.

**Slogans** fail because every business uses them. "Quality workmanship" can't help a customer choose, because everyone claims it.

**Jargon** fails because customers don't use it. A homeowner searches "fuse box keeps tripping", not "RCD nuisance tripping". If your page only uses trade terms, customers may not recognise it as the answer, and search engines may not match it to their search.

Both share the same underlying problem: they're written for the business, not the customer.

## Write like you talk

Here's the second question. You don't need to be a writer. You need to imagine a customer has rung you up and asked, "So what do you do?"

You wouldn't say "We provide bespoke heating solutions." You'd say something like:

"I fit and service boilers, mostly for homeowners in Barry and Penarth. If yours has packed up, I can usually come out the same week."

That's your website copy. Write it down.

Some rules of thumb:

- **Short sentences.** One idea each.
- **Everyday words.** "Use" not "utilise". "Help" not "facilitate".
- **Talk to "you"**, not "the customer".
- **Be specific.** Numbers, places, timescales.

## Swap every claim for a fact

Here's the third question. The quickest improvement is to find every empty claim and replace it with a specific fact.

| Empty claim | Specific fact |
| --- | --- |
| Quality workmanship | Every rewire is tested and certified, and you get the paperwork |
| Competitive prices | Boiler services from £X, with a reminder when the next one's due |
| No job too small | I'm happy to fix a single dripping tap |
| Reliable service | I'll give you a two-hour arrival window and text you if I'm running late |
| Years of experience | I've been fitting kitchens in the Vale of Glamorgan since 2012 |
| Fully insured | Public liability insurance up to £X |

Only use facts that are true for you, of course. But notice how much more each one tells a customer.

## Use the customer's words, then the trade term

People search the way they talk. So lead with their words, and add your trade term in brackets if it helps:

- "Fuse box upgrade (consumer unit replacement)."
- "Damp proofing for walls with black mould."
- "Garden rooms and summer houses."

You can find the words customers use in the questions they ask you, in your Google reviews, and in Google Search Console's list of searches.

Clear wording also helps AI search tools understand and describe your business. My guide on [getting mentioned in AI search answers](/blog/get-recommended-by-ai-search/) explains why.

## A before-and-after example

**Before:**

> "Jones Electrical are a professional electrical contractor providing a comprehensive range of high-quality domestic and commercial electrical services. We pride ourselves on our attention to detail and customer satisfaction."

**After:**

> "I'm Rob Jones, an electrician based in Barry. I do rewires, fuse box upgrades, extra sockets, outdoor lighting and EV charger installs for homes across the Vale of Glamorgan. You'll get a fixed written quote before I start, and I'll leave the place as tidy as I found it."

The second version is shorter, more human and far more useful, and every sentence helps a customer decide.

## The aha: cut the first paragraph

Many business owners write a warm-up paragraph before getting to the point: a welcome, a bit of history, a mission statement.

Try deleting your first paragraph and reading the page again. Often it's instantly better, because the real information was in the second paragraph all along.

## Where plain English matters most

- **The top of your home page**: what you do and where.
- **Each service page**: what's included, how it works, what it costs. My guide to [writing a services page](/blog/services-page-that-gets-enquiries/) gives the structure.
- **Your about page**: who you are and why you work the way you do. My guide to [the about page](/blog/about-page-for-small-business/) covers it.
- **Buttons**: "Get a free quote" beats "Submit".

## A quick editing routine

1. Read each page out loud.
2. Highlight every vague claim and replace it with a fact.
3. Swap trade jargon for customer words, with the trade term in brackets.
4. Delete the warm-up paragraph if it says nothing.
5. Ask a friend outside your trade to read it and tell you what you do.

If they can explain it back in one sentence, the page is working.
