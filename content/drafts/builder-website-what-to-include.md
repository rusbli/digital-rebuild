For a builder, a website isn't usually where the first contact happens. Most bigger jobs still come from word of mouth: a neighbour's extension, a friend's recommendation, your van parked outside.

But here's what happens next. The customer hears your name, then looks you up. Before they ever call, your website gets a few minutes to convince them you're the right person to trust with tens of thousands of pounds and months of disruption.

That's a very different job from an emergency plumber's site. Here's how to do it well.

> **Quick answer:** A builder's website should show finished projects in detail, with photos and a short write-up of each. It should explain how a job runs from first visit to handover, give honest guidance on costs and timescales, show genuine reviews and any accreditations you hold, and make it easy to book a visit. Lead with the type of work you want more of.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What convinces someone to trust a builder with a big project?
- How do you talk about cost when every job is different?
- How do you attract the jobs you actually want?

## Lead with the work you want

Here's the third question first, because it shapes everything else.

If you want more extensions and kitchen refits, and fewer small repair jobs, your website should make that obvious. Put the bigger projects front and centre. Give them the best photos and the most detail.

Websites attract more of whatever they show. A home page full of small repairs will bring more small repairs.

## Project pages: your strongest asset

Here's the first question. For bigger jobs, nothing builds trust like seeing finished work that looks like what the customer wants.

Rather than one gallery of random photos, create a page for each good project:

- **What the customer wanted.** "A single-storey rear extension to make a kitchen-diner."
- **What you did.** Groundwork, structure, roofing, kitchen fitting, whatever applies.
- **How long it took.**
- **Before, during and after photos.**
- **Where** (the town, not the address).
- **A review** from that customer, if they're happy to give one.

Ask customers' permission before photographing their home, and avoid anything identifying, like house numbers.

These pages also help with Google. A page about a kitchen extension in Penarth gives Google something specific to match when someone nearby searches for exactly that.

## Explain how a job runs

Customers planning big work are often nervous about the unknowns. Who does what? What happens if something goes wrong? How long will they be without a kitchen?

A clear process page reduces that anxiety:

1. **First visit.** You look at the space and talk through what they want.
2. **Quote.** A written, itemised quote within a set time.
3. **Planning and approvals.** What needs planning permission or building regulations approval, and who arranges it.
4. **Schedule.** Start date, rough timeline, and how you'll keep them updated.
5. **The build.** How you protect their home, manage dust and tidy up.
6. **Handover.** Final checks, snagging, guarantees and paperwork.

Be honest about building regulations. For many projects, approval or sign-off is required, and customers appreciate a builder who explains it rather than glossing over it. GOV.UK's guidance on [building regulations approval](https://www.gov.uk/building-regulations-approval) is a useful link for customers who want to read more.

## Talking about cost honestly

Here's the second question. Nobody can price an extension from a website. But you can help customers know whether they're in the right ballpark.

- **Explain what drives cost**: size, structure, finishes, access, the kitchen or bathroom spec.
- **Give ranges for typical projects** if you're comfortable doing so, based on your own recent work.
- **Explain your quoting process**: whether the first visit is free, how detailed the quote is, and how you handle changes.
- **Be upfront about contingencies.** Old houses hide surprises, and customers trust a builder who says so.

My guide on [whether to show prices on your website](/blog/should-you-show-prices-on-your-website/) explains why some guidance is almost always better than none.

## Trust signals for big jobs

- **Genuine reviews**, ideally mentioning the type of project.
- **Accreditations and memberships** you genuinely hold, with ways for customers to check them.
- **Insurance** you carry.
- **Guarantees** you offer on your work.
- **Who you work with**: your own team, trusted trades, how you manage them.
- **A real about page**, with photos of you and your team. My guide to [writing an about page](/blog/about-page-for-small-business/) covers what to include.

## Service pages for each type of project

Give each main type of work its own page: extensions, loft conversions, kitchen fitting, renovations, whatever you do most. Each page should answer the questions specific to that work, as my guide to [writing a services page](/blog/services-page-that-gets-enquiries/) explains.

## The contact page for bigger jobs

For larger projects, ask a few more useful questions up front:

- Type of project.
- Rough timescale.
- Whether they have plans or drawings yet.
- Postcode.
- Best way and time to contact them.

That helps you prioritise genuine, ready-to-go enquiries, and shows the customer you're organised.

## The aha: show the middle of the job

Most builders' galleries only show the finished result. But customers worry most about the **middle**: the mess, the disruption, the bit where their house is a building site.

A few honest "during" photos (protected floors, a tidy site, dust sheets in place) can be surprisingly persuasive. They show how you'll treat their home while you're working, which is exactly what they're nervous about.

## A quick checklist

1. Does the home page lead with the work you want more of?
2. Are there detailed project pages, with photos and a short write-up?
3. Is there a clear explanation of how a job runs?
4. Is there honest guidance on cost?
5. Are reviews, accreditations and insurance visible?
6. Is it easy to book a first visit?
