You cover Barry. And Penarth. And Dinas Powys, Llantwit Major, Cowbridge, Sully, Rhoose and half of Cardiff.

So should you have a separate page for each one?

You'll find plenty of advice saying yes: make a "Plumber in Penarth" page, a "Plumber in Cowbridge" page, and so on, and watch the rankings roll in.

The truth is more nuanced. Done well, a few location pages can help. Done badly (which is how most are done) they're a waste of time, or worse.

> **Quick answer:** Only create a page for a town if you can make it genuinely useful and different for people there: real local jobs, photos, reviews, response times and local details. Don't create near-identical pages with the town name swapped; Google's spam policies warn against pages made mainly to rank for similar searches. For many small businesses, clearly listing your service area and having a strong Google Business Profile matters more.

Three questions we'll answer:

- Why don't copy-and-paste town pages work?
- When is a location page worth writing?
- What should go on one?

## The copy-and-paste trap

Here's the first question. The classic approach looks like this:

> "Looking for a reliable plumber in Penarth? Our Penarth plumbers provide high-quality plumbing services across Penarth..."

Then the same page again for Cowbridge, Sully and Rhoose, with only the town name changed.

Those pages don't help customers. Someone in Penarth learns nothing they couldn't learn from your home page. And they don't fool Google either.

Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) describe **doorway abuse**: creating pages mainly to rank for specific, similar searches, which funnel visitors to the same place. Multiple near-identical pages targeting different towns is one of the examples Google gives.

So the risk isn't just wasted effort. Pages like that can do more harm than good.

## How local results really work

For many local searches, the most visible results are the map listings, which come from Google Business Profiles. Your profile's service area, categories and reviews do a lot of the work there. My guide to [showing up in near-me searches](/blog/how-to-show-up-in-near-me-searches/) explains those factors.

Your website supports that by clearly saying what you do and where. That means:

- Listing the towns and areas you cover, in plain words, on your home page and service pages.
- Making your business name, phone number and area consistent with your profile.

For lots of small businesses, that's enough.

## When a location page is worth writing

Here's the second question. A page for a specific town makes sense when:

- **You genuinely work there a lot**, and can show it.
- **You have something specific to say**: local jobs, local reviews, local factors.
- **People there search for your service with the town name**, and you can see that in Google Search Console.
- **The town is a meaningful place** for your customers, not a tiny hamlet on the edge of your map.

A good way to decide: open Google Search Console and look at the searches people use to find you. If a particular town keeps appearing, that's a sign a page for it could be useful. My [Search Console guide](/blog/google-search-console-for-small-business/) shows where to look.

## What to put on a location page

Here's the third question. A useful location page reads like it was written for people in that place:

- **Jobs you've done there.** Photos and a sentence about each: "New consumer unit in a Victorian terrace in Penarth."
- **Reviews from customers there.**
- **How you serve that area.** How quickly you can usually get there, which days you're nearby.
- **Local factors.** Older housing stock, hard water, parking restrictions, conservation areas: anything that genuinely affects the work.
- **The services you offer there**, linking to your main service pages.
- **A clear way to get in touch.**

If you can't fill a page with real, specific information like this, you don't need the page yet.

## The aha: depth beats breadth

Ten thin town pages tell Google you've copied yourself ten times. Three rich ones, each full of real local jobs and reviews, tell it you genuinely serve those places.

It's better to start with your two or three most important towns and do them properly. You can add more as you build up real local examples.

## A sensible structure for most local businesses

1. **Home page**: what you do and the areas you cover, listed in plain words.
2. **Service pages**: one for each main service, mentioning your area naturally. My guide to [writing a services page](/blog/services-page-that-gets-enquiries/) covers the structure.
3. **A small number of location pages**, only where you have real local content.
4. **A complete Google Business Profile** with an accurate service area.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Dozens of pages with the town name swapped.**
- **Pages for places you rarely go.**
- **Stuffing town names into every sentence.** It reads badly and helps nobody.
- **Fake addresses** in towns you don't have premises in.
- **Forgetting the call to action.** A location page still needs an easy way to get in touch.

## A quick test for any location page

Ask: "If I lived in this town, would this page tell me something useful that the home page doesn't?"

If the answer's yes, it's probably worth having. If not, put the effort into your Google Business Profile and your main service pages instead.
