Imagine trying to book a plumber when you can't easily read small grey text. Or when you use a screen reader that announces every image as "image". Or when you can't use a mouse, and the contact form won't let you tab through it.

For lots of people, that's everyday reality on badly built websites. They give up and go elsewhere.

Accessibility sounds technical, but the basics are mostly common sense, and many of the fixes make your site better for everyone.

> **Quick answer:** An accessible website is one more people can use, including disabled customers. Under the Equality Act 2010, service providers must make reasonable adjustments, and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the usual benchmark. Start with simple checks: readable text and good contrast, descriptive image alt text, clear link wording, labelled form fields, and the ability to use the site with a keyboard alone.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What does UK law say?
- What can I check myself?
- Which fixes help most?

## What UK law says

Here's the first question. Under the Equality Act 2010, service providers have a duty to make reasonable adjustments so disabled people aren't put at a substantial disadvantage. That's generally understood to apply to websites as well as premises.

GOV.UK's guidance on [accessibility requirements](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/accessibility-requirements-for-public-sector-websites-and-apps) sets out specific rules for public sector websites, and notes that all UK service providers have a legal obligation to make reasonable adjustments.

For private businesses, there isn't a specific technical standard written into law, but the [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) are widely used as the benchmark.

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. But the practical point is simple: making your website usable for more people is both the right thing to do and good for business.

## Checks you can do yourself

Here's the second question. No special tools needed.

### 1. Can you read it comfortably?

Is the text large enough on a phone? Is there strong contrast between text and background? Pale grey on white is a common problem.

### 2. Can you use it with just a keyboard?

On a computer, press the Tab key repeatedly. Can you reach every link, button and form field, and can you see where you are on the page?

### 3. Do images have descriptions?

Images need alt text: a short description for people using screen readers. "Walk-in shower with grey tiles" is useful. "IMG_4837" isn't.

### 4. Do links make sense on their own?

"Click here" and "read more" mean nothing to someone jumping between links with a screen reader. "Read our boiler servicing guide" does.

### 5. Are form fields labelled?

Every field should have a visible label that stays visible while typing, not just placeholder text that disappears.

### 6. Does zooming work?

Zoom your browser to 200%. Does the page still work without overlapping text or sideways scrolling?

### 7. Is colour the only signal?

If errors are shown only in red, or links only by colour, some people won't notice them.

## The fixes that help most

Here's the third question. On most small business websites, these make the biggest difference:

| Fix | Who it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Larger text and stronger contrast | People with low vision, everyone on a phone in sunlight |
| Alt text on images | Screen reader users, and search engines |
| Clear headings in order | Screen reader users, and skim readers |
| Descriptive link text | Screen reader users, everyone scanning |
| Labelled form fields | Everyone filling in your form |
| Keyboard access | People who can't use a mouse |
| Captions on videos | Deaf and hard-of-hearing people, people watching without sound |

## The aha: accessible usually means easier for everyone

Most accessibility fixes aren't special features for a few people. Clear text, sensible headings, obvious buttons, labelled forms and descriptive links make a website easier for everyone, including someone on a phone in bright sunlight, someone in a hurry, or someone reading in their second language.

Many of the same things also help search engines understand your pages.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Text in images**, which screen readers can't read.
- **Tiny, low-contrast text.**
- **Forms that rely only on placeholder text.**
- **Pop-ups that trap keyboard users.**
- **"Accessibility overlay" widgets** as a substitute for fixing the site itself. Fix the underlying problems.

## A quick checklist

1. Text readable, with strong contrast, on a phone?
2. The whole site usable with a keyboard?
3. Every meaningful image described with alt text?
4. Links that make sense out of context?
5. Form fields with visible labels?
6. Works when zoomed to 200%?
