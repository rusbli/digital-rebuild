Most small business websites have a contact form. Name, email, phone number, message.

That form is collecting personal information. And as soon as you collect personal information, UK data protection law expects you to tell people what you'll do with it.

That's what a privacy notice (often called a privacy policy) is for. It sounds like something only big companies need. In practice, most small business websites need a simple one.

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. But here's a plain guide, using the regulator's own resources.

> **Quick answer:** If your website collects any personal information, including through a contact form, booking tool or newsletter sign-up, you should have a privacy notice explaining what you collect, why, how long you keep it, who you share it with, and people's rights. Keep it short and accurate, link it from your footer and forms, and use the ICO's free tools to help write it. Check whether you need to pay the ICO's data protection fee too.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What actually counts as collecting personal data?
- What does a privacy notice need to say?
- Is there anything else I need to do besides the notice?

## What counts as personal data on a small website

Here's the first question. Personal data is information that identifies a person. On a typical small business website, that includes:

- Names, email addresses and phone numbers from contact forms.
- Addresses and postcodes from quote or booking forms.
- Details collected through booking or payment tools.
- Email addresses from newsletter sign-ups.
- Information collected by analytics or tracking tools.

If your website does any of these, you're collecting personal data, and people have a right to know what happens to it.

## What a privacy notice should cover

Here's the second question. The ICO's guidance on [the right to be informed](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/the-right-to-be-informed/) sets out what people should be told. In plain English, a small business privacy notice should cover:

| Section | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Who you are | Your business name and contact details |
| What you collect | The information each form or tool collects |
| Why | For example, to reply to enquiries, send quotes, carry out work |
| Your lawful basis | The legal reason you're allowed to use it (for most enquiries, this is often to take steps towards a contract, or legitimate interests) |
| Who you share it with | For example, your email provider, booking system or accountant |
| How long you keep it | A realistic period, such as how long you keep quotes and invoices |
| People's rights | Including the right to access, correct or delete their data, and to complain to the ICO |
| Cookies and tracking | What your site uses, or a link to your cookie information |

Keep it in plain English. A short, honest notice is far better than a long, copied one that doesn't describe what you actually do.

## Use the ICO's free help

The ICO has free resources for small organisations, including a tool to help you create your own privacy notice. Start at the ICO's [advice for small organisations](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/).

This is much safer than copying another business's policy, which will describe their data, their suppliers and their retention periods, not yours.

## Don't copy someone else's

It's tempting to borrow a policy from a bigger company's website. The problem is that it'll be wrong for you.

It might mention systems you don't use, data you don't collect, or countries you don't send data to. It might leave out the booking tool or email service you do use. An inaccurate notice doesn't do its job, and it can look careless.

## Where to put it

- **A link in your website footer**, on every page.
- **A short line next to each form**, such as: "I'll only use your details to reply about your enquiry. See the privacy notice."
- **In newsletter sign-ups**, with a clear explanation of what people are signing up for.

## Other things to check

Here's the third question. The privacy notice is only part of it.

### The data protection fee

Many organisations that process personal information must pay a yearly fee to the ICO unless they're exempt. The ICO's [data protection fee](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/data-protection-fee/) page has a short self-assessment to check whether you need to pay and how much. When I checked on 21 November 2026, the lowest tier for most micro-businesses was £52 a year, but use the ICO's own tool for your figure.

### Cookies

Cookies and similar technologies have their own rules. My guide on [whether you need a cookie banner](/blog/do-i-need-a-cookie-banner/) covers that separately.

### Keeping data safe

Store enquiries somewhere secure, use strong passwords and two-step verification on your email, and delete old data you no longer need. Your business email account holds a lot of personal information, so protect it well. My guide to [setting up business email on your own domain](/blog/business-email-with-your-own-domain/) covers keeping control of that account.

### Limited companies

If your business is a limited company, GOV.UK says your website must show certain company details, including your registered number and registered office address. See [GOV.UK's guidance on signs, stationery and promotional material](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material).

## The aha: collect less, and it all gets easier

The simplest privacy notice belongs to the website that collects the least.

Ask yourself whether you need every field on your forms. Do you need someone's full address to reply to an enquiry, or just a postcode? Do you need their date of birth at all?

Fewer fields means less to explain, less to protect, and, as a bonus, forms that more people actually fill in.

## A quick checklist

1. List everything your website collects, form by form and tool by tool.
2. Remove fields you don't need.
3. Write a short privacy notice using the ICO's tools.
4. Link it from your footer and every form.
5. Check whether you need to pay the ICO data protection fee.
6. Review it whenever you add a new form or tool.
