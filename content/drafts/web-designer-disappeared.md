You need a small change to your website. New prices, a new phone number, a photo that needs swapping. You email your web designer. No reply.

You try again. Then you call. The number doesn't connect. Their own website has gone.

Now you're worried. Who controls your website? What happens when the domain renewal is due? Could your site just disappear?

This happens more often than you'd think, and usually not out of any bad intent. People retire, get ill, change careers or simply stop trading. The good news is that in most cases you can get control back. Here's how.

> **Quick answer:** Don't panic, and don't change anything yet. First, find out who holds your domain, where your site is hosted, and which accounts you already have access to. Send a polite written request for access. If there's no response, contact your domain registrar and hosting company with evidence the business and domain are yours. Prioritise the domain, because if it lapses you could lose your web address and email.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What do I check first?
- How do I get control of my domain?
- What if I can't recover the website itself?

## Don't change anything yet

Resist the urge to move everything immediately. Changing settings, cancelling payments or pointing your domain elsewhere before you know what's what can take your website or email offline.

Gather information first.

## Step 1: find out what's where

Here's the first question. Your website is really several parts. Find out who controls each one:

| Part | How to find out |
| --- | --- |
| Domain name | Look for old renewal emails or invoices. Ask your accountant if the business pays for it. Try a WHOIS lookup to find the registrar. |
| Hosting | Look for hosting invoices. The registrar or your email settings may also show where the site points. |
| Email | Which company provides it? Can you log in? |
| Google Business Profile | Can you log in? Are you the owner? |
| Search Console and Analytics | Do you have access? |

Check your bank statements for any website-related payments. Those often reveal which companies are involved.

## Step 2: write to the designer

Send a short, polite email (and a letter if you have an address) asking for:

- The domain to be transferred into your name, or the login to the account it's in.
- Access to the hosting, or a copy of the website files.
- Ownership of any Google accounts linked to your business.

Keep a copy. If they respond, great. If not, you have a record that you tried.

## Step 3: secure your domain

Here's the second question. The domain is the priority, because it's your web address and often your email address too.

Find the **registrar** (the company where the domain is registered) and the **renewal date**.

- **If the domain is in your name** but in the designer's account, contact the registrar. They can usually help the registered holder regain access, after checking your identity.
- **If it's in the designer's name**, it's harder. Contact the registrar, explain the situation, and provide evidence the domain is used by your business, such as invoices, business registration and examples of it on your marketing. Registrars have processes for disputes, and outcomes depend on the evidence.

Note the renewal date. If it's coming up soon, act quickly.

## Step 4: recover or rebuild the website

Here's the third question. If you can get access to the hosting, you can usually recover the website files and move them.

If you can't:

- **Check whether your site is still online.** If it is, a new designer may be able to save a copy of its content and images.
- **Look for copies of your text and photos** in your own emails and files.
- **Consider a rebuild.** Sometimes starting fresh is faster than recovering an old site. My guide on [whether your website needs a rebuild](/blog/signs-your-website-needs-a-rebuild/) helps you decide.

If you rebuild, keep important page addresses the same where possible, or redirect them, so you don't lose whatever position the old site had in Google.

## Step 5: recover your Google accounts

- **Google Business Profile**: if you can't access it, Google has a process to request ownership of a profile.
- **Search Console**: you can verify ownership yourself once you control the domain.
- **Analytics**: you may need to set up a new account if the old one belonged to the designer.

## The aha: the problem started on day one

When a designer disappears, the real problem usually isn't the disappearance. It's how things were set up at the beginning: the domain in their name, the hosting in their account, no logins handed over.

That's why I make sure every client owns their domain, code and content from day one. It's not just good manners. It means a website survives whatever happens to the person who built it.

## How to stop it happening again

1. **Register your domain in your own name**, in an account you control. My guide to [choosing a domain name](/blog/choosing-a-domain-name-uk/) covers setting it up.
2. **Keep a list of every account and login**, stored safely in a password manager.
3. **Be the owner** of your Google Business Profile, Search Console and Analytics.
4. **Know where your site is hosted** and when things renew.
5. **Make sure a care plan doesn't lock you in.** My guide to [website care plans](/blog/website-care-plan-cost/) covers the questions to ask.
