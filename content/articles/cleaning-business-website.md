Hiring a cleaner is an oddly personal decision.

Someone will be in your home while you're out. They'll have your keys or your door code. They'll see the state of your kitchen on a bad week.

So when people look at a cleaning business website, they're asking two questions at once: "Can I afford this?" and "Can I trust these people in my house?"

A good cleaning website answers both, quickly. Here's what to include.

> **Quick answer:** A cleaning business website should say which areas you cover and what kinds of cleaning you do, right at the top. Show how pricing works, even if it's a starting price or hourly rate. Then answer the trust questions: who'll be cleaning, how you handle keys, what insurance you hold, what happens if something's missed. Add genuine reviews and a short quote form.

Three questions we'll answer:

- What makes someone trust a cleaner they've never met?
- How should cleaners show prices?
- Regular cleans or one-offs: which should the website lead with?

## Start with where and what

At the top of the home page, before scrolling:

- **What you do.** "Domestic cleaning", "end of tenancy cleaning", "office cleaning".
- **Where.** "Barry, Penarth and the Vale of Glamorgan."
- **The next step.** "Get a quote" or "Book a clean", plus a phone number.

If you do both homes and businesses, say so clearly, because they're different customers with different questions.

## Trust: the question behind every enquiry

Here's the first question. With cleaning, trust is often the deciding factor. Answer these openly:

### Who will actually be cleaning?

A photo of you or your team, with first names, makes a huge difference. If you work alone, say so: many customers love knowing the same person will come each time.

### How do you handle keys and access?

Explain how keys are stored, labelled and returned, or how you use key safes and door codes. It's a worry that stops people booking, and very few cleaning websites address it.

### What insurance do you hold?

If you have public liability insurance, say so. Customers want to know that an accident won't become their problem.

### Background checks

If you or your team hold background checks, say what kind. Only claim what's true.

### What happens if something's missed?

A simple promise, such as "If anything isn't right, tell us within 24 hours and we'll come back and sort it", reassures more than any adjective.

## Prices: show something

Here's the second question. People want a rough idea before they get in touch. Options that work for cleaners:

- **An hourly rate**, with a minimum number of hours per visit.
- **Typical prices for a regular clean**, by property size: "A two-bedroom flat, weekly, usually takes around two hours."
- **Fixed prices for one-off cleans** such as end of tenancy or after building work, with what's included.
- **Whether you bring products and equipment**, and if that changes the price.

Use your own real figures and update them when they change. My guide on [whether to show prices on your website](/blog/should-you-show-prices-on-your-website/) explains why a rough figure usually brings in better enquiries than none.

## Regular or one-off?

Here's the third question. Regular clients are the backbone of most cleaning businesses, but one-off jobs (end of tenancy, deep cleans, after building work) are often what people search for first.

A good approach:

- Lead the home page with your **main** type of work.
- Give each main service its own page: regular domestic cleaning, end of tenancy, deep cleans, office cleaning.
- On one-off pages, mention that you also offer regular cleans. A tenant who liked your end of tenancy clean might want you at their new place.

Separate pages answer each customer's specific questions and give Google something to match when someone searches for exactly that service. My guide to [writing a services page that brings in enquiries](/blog/services-page-that-gets-enquiries/) shows the structure.

## What each service page should cover

| Service | What customers want to know |
| --- | --- |
| Regular domestic cleaning | Same cleaner each time? How often? What's done each visit? |
| End of tenancy | Will it meet the letting agent's checklist? Ovens and carpets included? |
| Deep clean | What's different from a regular clean? How long does it take? |
| Office or commercial | Out of hours? Contracts? Insurance and staff vetting? |

A checklist of what's included in each type of clean is one of the most useful things you can add. It sets expectations and answers the question "what exactly do I get?"

## Reviews matter even more for cleaners

Because trust is so central, genuine reviews carry a lot of weight. Ask every new regular client after the first few cleans, when they've seen the difference. My guide to [getting more Google reviews the honest way](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/) covers how to ask without breaking Google's rules.

Show a few on your website, with first names and areas, and link to your Google reviews.

## A quote form that respects people's time

Ask only what you need to give a sensible price:

- Type of clean.
- Property type and number of bedrooms and bathrooms.
- Postcode.
- How often (for regular cleans).
- Preferred days.
- Contact details.

Add a line saying when you'll reply. Then test the form every month.

## Mistakes cleaning websites make

- **Stock photos of gleaming kitchens** that aren't yours.
- **No mention of keys, insurance or who'll be cleaning.**
- **"Call for a quote" with no figures anywhere.**
- **One page for every type of cleaning**, so nobody finds what they're looking for.
- **No area information**, so people don't know if you cover them.

## A quick checklist

1. Area and type of cleaning clear at the top?
2. Real photos of you or your team?
3. Keys, insurance and "what if something's missed" answered?
4. Some idea of price on each service page?
5. A checklist of what's included in each clean?
6. Genuine reviews, and a short quote form that works?

Get those right and your website will do much of the trust-building before you've even spoken to the customer.
