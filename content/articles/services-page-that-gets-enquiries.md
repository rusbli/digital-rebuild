Most small business services pages look the same.

A heading that says "Our Services". A grid of six boxes. Each box has an icon and one vague sentence: "We offer a complete range of bathroom solutions tailored to your needs."

Nobody's ever booked a job because of that sentence.

A services page has a specific job. It's where a customer goes once they're interested, to check you do exactly what they need, and to decide whether to get in touch. Here's how to write one that does that job.

> **Quick answer:** Give each main service its own page. Open with exactly what the service is and where you offer it. Then answer, in order, the questions customers ask: what's included, who it's for, how it works, what it costs, and why they should trust you. Add real photos, a short FAQ, and a clear way to get in touch near the top and at the end.

Three questions we'll answer:

- One services page, or one page per service?
- What should actually go on the page?
- How do I make it sound like me, not a brochure?

## One page per service, with a short overview

Here's the first question. If you offer three to eight main services, give each one its own page. Then have a short overview page (or a section on your home page) that lists them and links to each.

This does two things.

For customers, it means the person looking for a new boiler doesn't have to wade through paragraphs about bathroom tiling.

For Google, it means there's a page specifically about, say, boiler installation in the Vale of Glamorgan, which can appear when someone searches for exactly that.

My guide to [what to put on your homepage](/blog/small-business-homepage-what-to-include/) explains how the overview fits into your home page.

## The structure that works

Here's the second question. Write the page in the order a customer thinks.

### 1. Say exactly what it is, and where

The heading and first two lines should leave no doubt.

> "Boiler installation in Barry, Penarth and Cardiff. Gas Safe registered, fixed written quotes, most installs completed in a day."

That's more useful than any amount of "we pride ourselves on".

### 2. Who it's for

Describe the situations where someone needs this service. "Your boiler is over 15 years old and keeps breaking down" or "You're buying a house and need a survey" helps a reader recognise themselves.

### 3. What's included

List what the customer actually gets. This is where vague pages fall down. Be specific: what you supply, what you remove, what you tidy up, what guarantees apply.

### 4. How it works

Three to five steps, from first contact to finished job. People hesitate when they don't know what happens next, so take the mystery away.

### 5. What it costs

Starting prices, typical ranges, or how you quote. My guide on [whether to show prices on your website](/blog/should-you-show-prices-on-your-website/) explains why some figure is nearly always better than none.

### 6. Proof

Photos of this specific service, a review that mentions it, and any accreditation that applies to it.

### 7. A short FAQ

Three or four questions customers really ask you about this service, answered plainly.

### 8. The next step

A clear call to action: "Book a free survey", "Get a quote", "Call 07... now". Put it near the top as well, so nobody has to scroll to the bottom.

## Write it like you talk

Here's the third question. The easiest way to sound like yourself is to imagine a customer asking you about this service on the phone, and write down what you'd say.

You wouldn't say "we deliver bespoke solutions". You'd say "we'll come out, measure up, and send you a written price within two days".

A few rules of thumb:

- Use the words customers use. If they say "fuse box", say "fuse box" (and "consumer unit" alongside it if you like).
- Short sentences. Short paragraphs.
- Specific beats impressive. "Most installs take one day" beats "fast, efficient service".
- Talk to "you", not "the customer".

## The aha: answer the objection before it's raised

Every service has a reason people hesitate. A rewire is disruptive. A website feels expensive. A new kitchen means weeks without cooking.

The best services pages name that worry and answer it.

"A full rewire usually means two to three days of disruption. Here's how we keep you living in the house while we work."

Customers trust a business that's honest about the downsides. And you've answered the question that would otherwise have stopped them from getting in touch.

## Photos do half the work

For most services, a good photo of the finished result persuades faster than any paragraph. Use:

- Before-and-after pairs.
- Close-ups that show the quality of your work.
- Photos of you or your team doing the job.

Avoid stock photos. Customers can spot them, and they make everything else on the page feel less real.

## Common mistakes

- **One vague sentence per service.** Not enough to persuade or to rank.
- **The same text on every service page** with one word changed. It helps nobody.
- **No call to action**, or one hidden at the bottom.
- **Jargon** that only other people in your trade understand.
- **Listing every service you could possibly do.** Lead with the work you want more of.

## A quick check before you publish

Read the page on your phone and ask:

1. Within five seconds, do I know exactly what this service is and where it's offered?
2. Would a customer know what's included and roughly what it costs?
3. Is the worry that stops people from booking answered?
4. Is there a real photo of this work?
5. Is there an obvious way to get in touch, near the top and at the end?
